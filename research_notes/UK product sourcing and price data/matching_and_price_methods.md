## 1. Product entity matching: what the evidence says about WDC Products, Ditto/SBERT, cross-encoders, LLM judges, cascades, calibration and generic-versus-specific matching

### Takeaway
On consumer product benchmarks, zero-shot hosted LLMs reach or beat fine-tuned BERT-family matchers and are far more robust to unseen products, but corner-case (near-duplicate) pairs are the hardest slice, F1 swings by tens of points with prompt and model, and even the best LLM in the one cost-aware study still accepted about 0.8% of ambiguous non-matches. No source tests UK construction or MRO lines, and label noise of about 4% in a curated WDC test sample means public benchmarks cannot certify a sub-0.5% wrong-accept target.

### Cited Findings

**Benchmark design and label quality**
- **[V]** WDC Products (*peer-reviewed*, EDBT 2024) holds 11,715 offers for 2,162 entities from web shops; it has 27 variants built from three dimensions: corner-case share (80/50/20%), share of unseen products (0/50/100%) and development-set size (small/medium/large). Labels come from product-identifier clusters (MPN, GTIN, SKU) in schema.org markup; corner cases are pairs that resemble the other class (similar-looking non-matches, dissimilar-looking matches); hard negatives were picked with Cosine, DICE and Generalized Jaccard title similarity; the best systems scored "Top-F1 scores between 0.64 and 0.89 depending on the variant". **[consumer]** — [WDC Products paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2301.09521)
- **[V]** Same paper: two expert annotators checked a 600-pair sample of test pairs (300 positive, 300 negative) and estimated noise at 4.00% and 4.17% (Cohen's kappa 0.91). Corner cases hurt precision more than recall: at medium development size, 80% corner cases, seen products, RoBERTa had P 66.61 / R 79.07, Ditto P 70.97 / R 77.53, HierGAT P 68.68 / R 74.40, R-SupCon P 76.18 / R 84.20; the authors say this "points to the matching systems mistaking very similar negatives as positives". R-SupCon fell from F1 79.99 (seen) to 53.10 (unseen) in that setting. — [WDC Products paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2301.09521)
- **[V]** The WDC Products web page shows no licence or terms of use (it points users to a Google Group); the Spec also lists the licence as "Not stated". — [WDC Products page](https://webdatacommons.org/largescaleproductcorpus/wdc-products/)

**Hosted LLMs versus fine-tuned language-model matchers (Peeters, Steiner, Bizer)**
- **[V]** (*peer-reviewed*, EDBT 2025) On the WDC Products test set (80% corner cases; Table 1 lists 259 positive and 989 negative test pairs; small development set), best zero-shot-prompt F1 was GPT-4 89.61, GPT-4o 87.64, Llama-3.1-70B 83.67, GPT-4o-mini 81.15, Llama-2-70B 69.09, Mixtral 53.37, versus fine-tuned RoBERTa 77.53 and Ditto 84.90. **[consumer]** — [Entity Matching using LLMs](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.11244)
- **[V]** Same paper: fine-tuned RoBERTa/Ditto trained on another benchmark and applied to the WDC test set scored, for models trained on Abt-Buy / Walmart-Amazon / Amazon-Google / DBLP-Scholar / DBLP-ACM respectively, Ditto 48.74 / 31.55 / 33.12 / 32.82 / 29.00 and RoBERTa 55.52 / 36.46 / 31.00 / 29.64 / 16.25; "All LLMs achieve at least 8% F1 higher performance than the best transferred PLM while GPT4 outperforms the best PLM by 40% to 68%." — [Entity Matching using LLMs](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.11244)
- **[V]** Prompt sensitivity on WDC across 10 prompt variants (Table 2): GPT-4 ranged 77.39 to 89.61 (SD 3.42); GPT-4o-mini ranged 16.85 to 81.15 (mean 51.69, SD 27.96); GPT-4o ranged 21.91 to 87.64 (mean 56.84, SD 25.66). The authors conclude there is "no single best prompt" and it must be tuned per model/dataset. — [Entity Matching using LLMs](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.11244)
- **[V]** Few-shot demonstrations are not reliably helpful (Table 8, WDC best prompts, zero-shot -> 6-shot -> 10-shot F1): GPT-4o-mini 81.15 -> 67.48 -> 73.23; GPT-4 89.61 -> 87.23 -> 86.72; GPT-4o 87.64 -> 89.96 -> 91.74. Text: GPT-4's WDC performance "drops irrespective of the demonstration selection method"; GPT-mini and Mixtral lose "between 4 and 26%" on most datasets; GPT-4/4o do best with "related" demonstrations chosen by Generalized Jaccard similarity; "the usefulness of in-context learning cannot be assumed but needs to be determined experimentally". — [Entity Matching using LLMs](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.11244)
- **[V]** Cost anchors in the same paper (August 2024 prices per M prompt/completion tokens: GPT-4o-mini $0.15/$0.60, GPT-4 $30/$60, GPT-4o $2.50/$10): a zero-shot pair prompt averaged 76-93 prompt tokens, 10-shot prompts 942-1,009 tokens; cost per prompt 0.006 cents (mini), 0.024 cents (4o), 0.474 cents (GPT-4); fine-tuned GPT-4o-mini reached F1 88.89 on WDC at 0.003 cents per inference prompt. — [Entity Matching using LLMs](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.11244)
- **[V]** GPT-4's own structured explanations of wrong Walmart-Amazon decisions were clustered into error classes. False positives: "Overemphasis on Matching Attributes" (brand, model number), "Ignoring Minor but Significant Differences", "Misinterpretation of Accessory or Variant Information", "Price Discrepancy Overlooked", "Condition or Quality Differences"; false negatives: model-number mismatch, missing attribute, minor description differences, price differences, variant/accessory differences. **[consumer]** — [Entity Matching using LLMs](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.11244)
- **[V]** (*preprint*, v2 May 2025) Fine-tuning "significantly improves the performance of the smaller models while the results for the larger models are mixed"; it improves in-domain generalisation but hurts cross-domain transfer; structured explanations in the training set help 3 of 4 LLMs. — [Fine-tuning LLMs for Entity Matching](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.08185)

**Cross-encoders, bi-encoders and small models**
- **[V]** Ditto (*preprint*): a pre-trained transformer fine-tuned as a sequence-pair classifier; it "outperforms previous state-of-the-art (SOTA), by up to 29% of F1 score"; three optimisations (domain-knowledge highlighting, summarising long strings, data augmentation with difficult examples) add "up to 9.8%"; it matches earlier SOTA with "at most half the number of labeled data". — [Ditto](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2004.00584)
- **[V]** Sentence-BERT (*preprint*): finding the most similar pair among 10,000 sentences needs "about 50 million inference computations (~65 hours)" with a BERT cross-encoder and about 5 seconds with SBERT embeddings. — [Sentence-BERT](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1908.10084)
- **[V]** "Beyond Scale and Generation" (*preprint*, 27 Jul 2026; 1,215 fine-tuning runs; Qwen3 backbones; nine datasets): cross-encoders keep a consistent advantage over bi-encoders even at 8B; embedding-oriented checkpoints are what make bi-encoders work (macro F1 77.9 vs 53.7 for instruct and 57.4 for base variants; +20.3, p = 0.004); generative matchers "do not universally outperform cross-encoders", their edge is under distribution shift; "larger models tend to rely more heavily on shortcut learning". Practitioner guidance in the paper: "a medium-scale cross-encoder is a strong default", bi-encoders become competitive "when the representations can be cached and reused", API inference suits low-volume or changing workloads and fine-tuning cost "can be amortized after a few thousand API calls under the pricing and hardware assumptions" used. — [Beyond Scale and Generation](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.24688)
- **[V]** Same paper, shortcut-learning case (a movie title/director dataset, **[non-product]**): negative pairs with identical titles were 0.5% and 0.4% of training and validation negatives but rose more than 70-fold in the test set; cross-encoder precision fell from 96.9 (0.6B) to 76.4 (8B) while recall stayed near 99; rebalancing the title-collision rate restored F1 to about 98 at all sizes. — [Beyond Scale and Generation](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.24688)
- **[V]** AnyMatch (*preprint*): a small fine-tuned language model whose average prediction quality is "within 4.4% of the state-of-the-art method MatchGPT with the proprietary trillion-parameter model GPT-4", with "four orders of magnitude less parameters" and a "3,899 times lower inference cost (in dollars per 1,000 tokens)"; second-highest F1 overall against thirteen baselines on nine benchmarks. **[consumer]** — [AnyMatch](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.04073)

**LLM judge formats: match, compare, select; position bias**
- **[V]** "Match, Compare, or Select" (*preprint*, v3 Dec 2024; 8 datasets, 10 LLMs): comparing beats independent matching by 10.7% F1 on average; selecting (pick the match from a list) adds 5.32% over comparing and averages "16.02% improvement in F1 over the current matching strategy"; selecting costs "less than half of the matching strategy" because it avoids resending the anchor record and instructions n-1 times. But "selection accuracy varies significantly as the position of the matching record increases in the candidate list". Their ComEM pipeline first ranks candidates with a cheap local model (Flan-T5-XL, keeping the top 4) and then lets a stronger LLM select, giving "2~18% F1 improvements while spending less" than the single strategies. **[consumer]** — [Match, Compare, or Select](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2405.16884)
- **[V]** (*peer-reviewed*, ICLR 2024; 20 LLMs, MMLU) LLMs have a "selection bias" toward particular option positions: moving the gold answer to position D cut gpt-3.5-turbo from 67.2 to 60.9; with balanced answers llama-30B picked A/B/C/D 34.6/27.3/22.3/15.8% of the time. Their debiasing (PriDe) estimates the position prior by permuting option contents. **[non-product]** — [LLMs are not robust multiple-choice selectors](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2309.03882)

**Cost-aware LLM judge evidence on ambiguous product pairs**
- **[V]** Lin 2026 (*preprint*, Zenodo, CC BY 4.0, single independent author; the file the Spec cites): WDC Products test split, categories cameras, computers, shoes and watches; "ambiguous set" = 2,518 pairs (1,719 non-matches, 799 matches) where the author's tuned fuzzy matcher was uncertain. Fixed fuzzy rule F1 0.445 (597 FP, 399 FN); logistic regression on four similarity features F1 0.596. Claude Sonnet 4.6: accuracy/precision/recall/F1 0.976/0.982/0.941/0.961, 14 FP, 47 FN, $3.95 per 1,000 pairs. Claude Haiku 4.5: 0.974/0.980/0.937/0.958, 15 FP, 50 FN, $1.30. Gemini 2.5 Flash-Lite: 0.928/0.953/0.812/0.877, 32 FP, 150 FN, $0.086. Bootstrap 95% CIs for F1: Sonnet 0.951-0.971, Haiku 0.948-0.968, Flash-Lite 0.857-0.895. Opus was dropped because "it tied Haiku in the pilot while costing more". **[consumer]** — [Lin 2026 (Zenodo record)](https://zenodo.org/records/20089436) and [PDF](https://zenodo.org/records/20089436/files/Lin-LLM-Dedupe-Comparison-Study.pdf)
- **[V]** Same study, method details: one conservative prompt for all models, JSON output (decision, confidence, explanation, evidence field); the prompt told models that "differences in size, model number, specifications, color, quantity, bundle composition, or product variant could indicate distinct products"; costs use API-reported token counts at list prices with no batch or caching discounts; all three LLMs made more false negatives than false positives, which the author attributes to the conservative prompt. The study's own limitations: ambiguous pairs only (not full-distribution performance), four categories of one benchmark, single prompt, self-reported confidence. — [Lin 2026 PDF](https://zenodo.org/records/20089436/files/Lin-LLM-Dedupe-Comparison-Study.pdf)
- **[V]** Same study, verbalised confidence: expected calibration error 0.062 (Sonnet), 0.088 (Haiku), 0.346 (Flash-Lite); Haiku at confidence threshold 0.90 gave precision 0.985 / recall 0.836 (678 predicted matches), at 0.95 precision 1.000 / recall 0.447 (357 predicted matches); Sonnet at 0.90 gave 1.000 / 0.313 and at 0.95 gave 1.000 / 0.078. The author says confidence is "useful as a routing signal but imperfectly calibrated". — [Lin 2026 PDF](https://zenodo.org/records/20089436/files/Lin-LLM-Dedupe-Comparison-Study.pdf)
- **[V]** Independent-author preprint (Aug 2026) on Abt-Buy (2,194 labelled pairs): a simple rule-based baseline F1 0.950 versus LLM zero-shot 0.948; a few-shot prompt revision that "looked effective on a small validation sample" cut full-scale F1 to 0.914; across 200 pairs x 5 runs at temperature 0.7 the model agreed with itself 99.7% of the time and majority voting added only 0.005 F1 at 5x the cost. **[consumer]** — [When Do LLMs Actually Help With Data Quality?](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.18158)
- **[V]** OpenSanctions Pairs (*preprint*, 755,540 expert-labelled sanctions-entity pairs): rule-based matcher F1 91.3%, GPT-4o 99.0%, DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-14B 98.2%; "rules over-match, while LLMs struggle with cross-script transliteration". **[non-product]** — [OpenSanctions Pairs](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.11051)

**Cascades and routing (mostly non-matching tasks)**
- **[V]** FrugalGPT (*preprint*): an LLM cascade "can match the performance of the best individual LLM (e.g. GPT-4) with up to 98% cost reduction or improve the accuracy over GPT-4 by 4% with the same cost". **[non-product]** — [FrugalGPT](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.05176)
- **[V]** UCCI (*preprint*, May 2026): calibration-first cascade routing on a production NER workload of 75,000 queries cut cost by 31% (95% CI 27-35%) at micro-F1 0.91 and reduced ECE from 0.12 to 0.03, beating entropy thresholding, split-conformal routing and a FrugalGPT-style threshold; the authors argue most deployed routers use "uncalibrated confidence scores". **[non-product]** — [UCCI](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.18796)
- **[V]** Early abstention in cascades (*preprint*): across six benchmarks, allowing small models to abstain traded a 4.1% average rise in abstention for a 13.0% cost cut and a 5.0% error cut. **[non-product]** — [Cost-Saving LLM Cascades with Early Abstention](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.09054)

**Calibration and statistical guarantees**
- **[V]** (*preprint*, Oct 2025) Calibration of an entity-matching RoBERTa on Abt-Buy, DBLP-ACM, iTunes-Amazon and Company: baseline ECE 0.0043 to 0.0552 (slightly overconfident); temperature scaling cut ECE by up to 23.83%. This studies a fine-tuned encoder, not generative LLMs, and **I found no paper applying conformal prediction to entity matching**. — [Confidence Calibration in LLM-Based Entity Matching](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.19557)
- **[V]** Selective classification with a risk guarantee (Geifman and El-Yaniv, *preprint*): the SGR procedure picks a confidence threshold on labelled data so that "test error will be no larger than r* with probability" 1-delta; example: "2% error in top-5 ImageNet classification can be guaranteed with probability 99.9%, and almost 60% test coverage". **[non-product]** — [Selective Classification for Deep Neural Networks](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1705.08500)
- **[V]** Learn then Test (*preprint*): calibrates a model parameter (for example a selection threshold) to satisfy a user-chosen risk with finite-sample guarantees by testing multiple hypotheses with Hoeffding-Bentkus p-values and fixed-sequence testing; it lists selective classification with accuracy control as an application and says that for that binary loss "we use the exact binomial tail bound". **[non-product]** — [Learn then Test](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.01052)
- **[V]** Conformal risk control (*preprint*) bounds an expected loss, E[loss] <= alpha, i.e. a marginal guarantee averaged over calibration and test draws; conformal prediction with LLMs for multiple-choice QA found conformal uncertainty "tightly correlated with prediction accuracy" and stressed the exchangeability assumption (it uses model option probabilities). **[non-product]** — [Conformal Risk Control](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2208.02814); [Conformal Prediction with LLMs for MCQA](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.18404)

**Generic specification versus specific SKU (match groups)**
- **[V]** schema.org defines ProductGroup as "a group of Products that vary only in certain well-described ways, such as by size, color, material etc.", with `variesBy` naming the varying properties and `hasVariant` the members. *trade/ref* — [schema.org ProductGroup](https://schema.org/ProductGroup)
- **[V]** Amazon's Shopping Queries dataset labels (query, product) pairs Exact / Substitute / Complement / Irrelevant (about 130 thousand queries, 2.6 million judgements; a Substitute "fails to fulfill" some aspect but can serve as a functional substitute). **[consumer]** — [ESCI shopping queries dataset](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2206.06588)
- **[V]** WDC Products defines "same product" by shared MPN/GTIN/SKU identifier clusters, not by specification equivalence. — [WDC Products paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2301.09521)

### Inferences
- The Spec's red-team line "published results don't transfer" is right: every number above is consumer electronics, publications or sanctions data. Use them as method evidence (what fails, what costs what), not accuracy forecasts for plasterboard, timber or valves.
- With roughly 4% label noise in a curated WDC test sample, WDC can sanity-check that the judge behaves sensibly (the Spec's one-off benchmark check) but cannot support a "wrong auto-accept below 0.5%" claim; only the team's own double-labelled gold set can.
- Lin's counts imply that on ambiguous pairs even the best LLMs falsely accepted about 0.8-0.9% of true non-matches (Sonnet 14/1,719 = 0.81%, Haiku 15/1,719 = 0.87%, Flash-Lite 32/1,719 = 1.86%) and that roughly 1.8-2.0% of the pairs they called matches were wrong (Sonnet about 14 of ~766, Haiku about 15 of ~764) **[E: arithmetic on the cited counts]**. That is the empirical reason to keep the deterministic attribute check as the final authority, as the Spec already does.
- Cheap-versus-mid-tier model choice is a measurable quantity on the team's gold set: Lin found Haiku within 0.003 F1 of Sonnet at 0.33x the cost, but Peeters/Bizer show small-model behaviour can swing by 60 F1 points with prompt wording and can get worse with demonstrations. The Spec's "3 nearest approved matches" few-shot design therefore needs an A/B against zero-shot per model.
- Reading the Spec's model-choice sentence against the source: in Lin the "top-tier" comparator is Sonnet 4.6 (Opus tied Haiku in a pilot and was dropped), and the dollar figures are per 1,000 pairs of two full product records, not per 1,000 order lines with five candidates and three examples; the "about a third of the cost" ratio (1.30/3.95 = 0.33) transfers more safely than the absolute dollars.
- "Pick one of 5" judging is supported by the select-versus-match evidence (cheaper and better on average) but is exposed to position bias; run two orderings and treat disagreement as "review".
- Verbalised confidence and re-sampling are weak uncertainty signals (self-agreement 99.7%, ECE up to 0.346). Use deterministic signals (failed attribute checks, scorer/judge disagreement, permutation disagreement) for routing, and calibrate the final auto-accept threshold on labelled data with a binomial-bound procedure (SGR or Learn-then-Test style) rather than trusting raw scores.
- Match semantics: a generic line ("12.5mm tapered plasterboard 2.4x1.2") denotes a set (every SKU that satisfies the stated attributes), a branded line denotes a brand-restricted subset, and an MPN line denotes an identifier-defined single entity. These are different equivalence relations; modelling them as ProductGroup-like parents with `variesBy` attributes (thickness, width, length, edge type, grade, pack) makes the "must validate" attribute list explicit and lets one approval generalise to the group.

### Gaps
- No study isolates LLM-judge error on pairs that differ only in size, grade or pack (12.5 vs 15 mm, C16 vs C24, S1 vs S2) in construction or MRO products, and none uses UK data; the closest proxies are WDC corner cases and Lin's ambiguous set.
- The Spec cites LLM-MSMA (MDPI Buildings) as construction-domain evidence; the page returned HTTP 403 so I could not read its numbers.
- I could not find conformal-prediction work specific to entity matching or record linkage (the one search that touched it returned only a patent and the RoBERTa calibration paper).
- Lin's ambiguous set is defined by the author's own fuzzy matcher, so its numbers are not comparable with Peeters/Bizer's; it has one prompt, one run per model and no peer review.
- Whether hosted Claude models expose token log-probabilities (needed for logit-based conformal methods) was not checked.

## 2. Normalisation: UK trade abbreviations and units, dimension chains, thread sizes, grade codes, and unit-parsing libraries versus hand-written parsers

### Takeaway
No library I tested parses the shapes that matter here ("2.4x1.2", "20 x 12.5mm", "4.0x40", "C16", "S1", "p/board"): Pint raises errors on them and quantulum3 silently drops or mis-splits them, so a small hand-written grammar plus a data-driven glossary is the right core; the trade vocabulary itself (CLS, PAR/PSE, TE/SE, nominal versus finished sizes) is documented only in merchant pages, forums and reference sites that disagree with each other.

### Cited Findings

**Trade vocabulary and size conventions**
- **[V]** Plasterboard edge types: "tapered edge, where the long edges of the board are tapered with a wide bevel at the front to allow jointing materials to be finished flush" versus "plain edge, used where the whole surface will receive a thin coating (skim coat)"; in Europe sheets are mostly 120 cm wide, most commonly 240 cm long, thickness 9.5 to 25 mm. The article says "plain edge" where UK merchants say "square edge". *trade/ref* — [Wikipedia: Drywall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drywall)
- **[U]** UK merchant listing titles seen in search results write the same board in different dimension orders: "Knauf Tapered Edge Plasterboard - 12.5 x 1200 x 2400mm" (thickness x width x length) and "12.5mm Standard Plasterboard Tapered Edge 2400 x 1200mm (8' x 4')" (length x width with imperial equivalent). *trade/ref* — [Wickes tapered edge listing](https://www.wickes.co.uk/Knauf-Tapered-Edge-Plasterboard---12-5-x-1200-x-2400mm/p/220210); [Wickes square edge listing](https://www.wickes.co.uk/Knauf-Square-Edge-Plasterboard---12-5-x-1200-x-2400mm/p/104123); [Sheet Materials Wholesale](https://sheetmaterialswholesale.co.uk/12-5mm-standard-plasterboard-tapered-edge-2400-x-1200mm/)
- **[V]** A UK timber merchant: "Canadian Lumber Standard (CLS) is a structural timber"; it says CLS "is PAR or Planed All Round", "C16 graded", and writes titles with nominal size, finished size in brackets and an imperial family name: "CLS Timber FSC 50 x 75 x 3000mm (Finished Size 38x63mm) 3x2". The same page says finished size "will usually be 5mm less than stated" although its own example loses 12 mm on each face (50 to 38, 75 to 63), so merchant copy is internally inconsistent. *trade/ref* — [Howarth Timber](https://www.howarth-timber.co.uk/cls-timber-fsc-38-x-63-x-3000mm)
- **[V]** A builders' forum (low reliability): "PAR/PSE (planed all round/planed square edged) ... usually without treatment and square corners"; "you usually allow max 6mm to achieve par or pse. They both mean the same thing"; "4x2 regularised ... planed on all sides with the corners rounded, will usually end up at about 90mmx45mm"; "4x2 CLS ... usually around 87mmx 38mm". *trade/ref* (forum) — [BuildHub timber sizes thread](https://forum.buildhub.org.uk/topic/23-timber-sizes/)
- **[V]** A quoting-app knowledge page tabulates nominal / regularised / PAR sizes (47x100 nominal -> 45x97 regularised -> 44x95 PAR; 38x89 CLS) but also says PAR is "~3-5mm smaller than nominal" while its own example is 6 mm (50 to 44). *trade/ref* — [squote timber sizes](https://squote.app/knowledge/cross-reference/timber-sizes)
- **[V]** Strength classes: "For softwoods, the common classes are (in increasing strength) C16, C18, C24, and C30"; European strength grading is to EN 14081 with classes from EN 338. *trade/ref* — [Wikipedia: Dimensional lumber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimensional_lumber)
- **[V]** OSB grades under EN 300: OSB/1 general-purpose and interior fitments (dry), OSB/2 load-bearing (dry), OSB/3 "Load-bearing boards for use in humid conditions", OSB/4 heavy-duty load-bearing (humid). *trade/ref* — [Wikipedia: Oriented strand board](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oriented_strand_board)
- **[V]** Tile adhesive classification (EN 12004): C1 normal and C2 improved (adhesion strength at least 0.5 MPa and 1 MPa respectively), S1 flexible and S2 highly flexible, plus T (reduced slip), E (extended open time), F (fast setting); S1 is "mandatory" for porcelain with very low water absorption, larger formats and critical substrates such as gypsum plasterboard and floor heating; codes concatenate (for example "C2TE S1"). *docs* (manufacturer) — [Wacker tile adhesive brochure](https://www.wacker.com/h/medias/7943-EN.pdf)
- **[V]** Trade press gives the S1/S2 deformability limits as S1 "between 2.5mm and 5mm" and S2 "above 5mm", and says they were introduced to the UK standard in "BS EN 12004:2007 + A1 2012 as ... optional characteristics". *trade/ref* — [Tile and Stone Journal](https://www.tileandstonejournal.com/help-and-advice/understanding-tile-adhesive-classifications-including-s1-or-s2-deformation/)
- **[V]** Copper tube to EN 1057: "Common sizes are 15 mm and 22 mm" (outside diameters), so "15mm" on a valve names the pipe it fits, not a body size. *trade/ref* — [Wikipedia: Copper tubing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper_tubing)
- **[V]** ISO metric thread designation: "M" + nominal diameter and pitch in mm (M8x1.25), coarse pitch omitted (M8), bolt length after a multiplication sign (M8x30). *trade/ref* — [Wikipedia: ISO metric screw thread](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ISO_metric_screw_thread)

**Unit and quantity parsing libraries**
- **[V]** Pint: BSD licence, "minimal dependencies" (0.26.1 needs Python 3.12+; flexcache, flexparser, platformdirs, typing-extensions), 0.27-0.33 MB artefacts; its tutorial documents parsing of simple expressions (`ureg('2.54 * centimeter')`, `Q_('2.54cm')`) and an `UndefinedUnitError` for unknown units and says nothing about compound dimension strings. *docs* — [Pint on PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/Pint/); [Pint tutorial](https://pint.readthedocs.io/en/stable/getting/tutorial.html)

### Inferences
- **Own measurements [E]** (Pint 0.25.3, quantulum3 0.10.0, run on the example lines): Pint parses clean tokens ("12.5mm", "20kg", "310ml", "2.4m") but raises `UndefinedUnitError` on "2.4x1.2", "20 x 12.5mm", "4.0x40", "C16", "S1", "M6x40", "p/board", "20 sheets" and "1200 x 2400mm"; its default registry defines `bag` as 42.6376828 kg, so "25 kg bag" parses to `kilogram * bag`. quantulum3 returned [20, 12.5 mm, 2] for "20 sheets 12.5mm tapered p/board 2.4x1.2" (the "2.4x1.2" became a stray "2"), [20, 12.5 mm] for "20 x 12.5mm" (no quantity-versus-size decision), nothing for "4.0x40", nothing for "M6x40", only the 6 from "C16 CLS 38x63x2.4m 6 lengths", and converted "310ml" to cubic centimetre.
- A hand-written parser should be a small tokenizer plus grammar that emits typed candidates (`Measure(value: Decimal, unit, role)`), keeps every plausible reading of ambiguous chains, and never guesses: "N x size" ("20 x 12.5mm") stays a quantity-versus-size ambiguity routed to review (as the Spec says), three-number chains need a role order (thickness-width-length versus length-width-thickness), and metre-valued pairs ("2.4x1.2") must be disambiguated by category priors (sheet goods) or flagged.
- Units, trade synonyms (p/board, TE/SE, CLS/PAR/PSE, kg bag, ml cartridge) and size-equivalence tables belong in reviewed data files with provenance, not code, matching the Spec's "no hand-written product rules" stance (LLM proposes, people approve). Because merchants disagree about nominal versus finished sizes, store nominal and finished size as separate attributes and treat "38x63 CLS" = "50x75 nominal CLS" = "3x2 CLS" as an approved equivalence class rather than a numeric tolerance.
- Pint is acceptable for conversion factors (mm/m, ml/l, kg/g) behind a thin wrapper or only in dev tests, but should not be the runtime parser and its default registry should not be trusted for pack words ("bag", "box"). quantulum3 is not worth its 10.6 MB and optional GloVe classifier for this vocabulary.
- Grade tokens (C16, C24, OSB/3 written "OSB3", S1/S2, C1/C2, "TE", "SE") are categorical attributes with their own families; they must be compared by equality or an explicit "at least" rule, never by string similarity (see section 3 measurements, where C16 vs C24 scored 0.81-0.94 on every lexical metric).

### Gaps
- No authoritative UK trade glossary was found for "p/board", "ml cartridge", "kg bag", "4.0x40" screw notation or PSE (only a forum for PAR/PSE); the primary standards (BS EN 338, 300, 520, 12004, 1057, 14081) were not obtained, so every trade definition above is second-hand.
- No source on how UK merchants express pack versus unit (price per sheet, per pallet, per pack of N) or on "N x size" notation conventions; I did not locate Open Food Facts-style multipack parsing documentation.
- The UCUM page was only partly read, so "no trade packaging units" is an absence claim from about 38% of the text.
- A direct test of the Travis Perkins timber page failed (the fetched page body was empty), so no first-party UK merchant data model was inspected.

## 3. Fuzzy search and retrieval implementations: rapidfuzz, Jaro-Winkler, trigram and TF-IDF/BM25, hashed embeddings, sentence-transformer models, ANN libraries, Postgres (pg_trgm, pgvector) and search servers

### Takeaway
Top-k lexical retrieval (BM25/TF-IDF over words and character 3-grams) is a strong, cheap first stage for short product titles (Sparkly: recall 98.7-100% at k = 50 on 15 benchmark datasets), dense embeddings add synonym recall but are close to blind to numbers, and every string metric I measured rates wrong-size, wrong-grade or wrong-pack siblings at 0.76-0.97 while two true matches written in a different format score only 0.27-0.74; retrieval should therefore be hybrid and recall-oriented, with size, grade and pack decided symbolically downstream.

- Consequence for design: use lexical and vector similarity only to generate candidates (recall), never as evidence of sameness; encode numeric and grade facts as typed columns and check them symbolically (this also compensates for the near-chance numeric behaviour of small embedding models).
- **Offline/test implementation (pure Python, deterministic):** a ~40-line BM25 over (a) glossary-normalised word tokens, (b) character 3-grams of the title (as Sparkly does) and (c) canonical numeric/grade tokens such as `t:12.5`, `w:1200`, `grade:c16`, plus a hashed character-n-gram TF-IDF cosine built with `zlib.crc32` or `hashlib` (never built-in `hash()`, which is randomised per process), fused with a single convex weight tuned on the gold set (RRF with k = 60 as the zero-training fallback). Category filters from the ontology apply before scoring. `rank-bm25`/`bm25s` need only numpy and are optional; RapidFuzz is optional too (it has a pure-Python fallback module, `rapidfuzz.fuzz_py`; **[E]** top-50 `token_set_ratio` over 20,000 synthetic strings took about 25 ms with the compiled backend and about 363 ms with the Python fallback).
- **Production upgrade path:** Postgres as the retrieval store. Use `pg_trgm` (GiST for `ORDER BY <->` top-k, or GIN plus `%` with a lowered threshold) as one candidate generator; add BM25 through a Postgres BM25 extension (pg_textsearch has the permissive licence; ParadeDB is AGPL for the community edition) or application-side bm25s because native `ts_rank` has no IDF; add pgvector HNSW over a small permissively licensed embedding model (bge-small-en-v1.5 at 33.4M parameters or all-MiniLM-L6-v2 at 22.7M) with iterative scans or per-category partial indexes because HNSW filters are applied after the index scan; fuse in SQL with RRF or a tuned convex combination; keep numeric/grade attributes in typed columns. A cross-encoder reranker (for example the 22.7M-parameter ms-marco MiniLM, Apache-2.0) fine-tuned on approvals is the next step before widening LLM use. For catalogues of tens of thousands of SKUs exact pgvector search is plausibly adequate (an assumption to measure); hnswlib adds an install-time compiler dependency and FAISS adds a large wheel.
- Search servers (Typesense GPL-3.0, Meilisearch MIT plus BUSL parts, OpenSearch Apache-2.0, Elasticsearch AGPL/SSPL/ELv2) all need a running process, so they cannot back pure-Python unit tests; they make sense only if a typo-tolerant search UI is wanted, and Typesense's GPL needs legal review before embedding in a commercial product.
- `token_set_ratio`, `WRatio` and Jaro-Winkler are poor scoring features here (subset = 100; Jaro-Winkler is designed for short personal names); keep them, if at all, as low-weight candidate-generation features. TF-IDF-weighted token similarity with character-level soft matching (the Cohen et al. winner) is the better lexical pattern for product titles.

- No published benchmark of any embedding model, BM25 variant or search server on short UK trade titles; MTEB, ESCI-based retrieval scores and per-model latency were not collected.
- No recall-versus-latency data for pgvector HNSW/IVFFlat or pg_trgm at the team's catalogue size; the README gives defaults, not measurements.
- I did not verify whether managed Postgres services the team might use offer pgvector, pg_textsearch or ParadeDB.
- Typesense and Meilisearch quality on noisy product titles was not found.

## 4. Best-price methods: unit-price normalisation, pack-size rounding, landed cost, VAT basis, basket optimisation, staleness, outlier checks against indices, source-trust weighting

### Takeaway
Multi-merchant basket optimisation with delivery fees and free-delivery thresholds is strongly NP-hard in general but exactly solvable in seconds at 5-15 lines and 3-6 merchants (section 8); the weaker links are the inputs. For unit prices, VAT basis, pack rounding, price staleness and source trust I found only analogues (consumer unit-price law, ONS price-collection outlier rules, Google Shopping feed rules, truth-discovery methods) and no UK builders'-merchant data.

### Cited Findings

**Unit price, VAT, delivery**
- **[V]** The Price Marking Order 2004 (UK) obliges retailers to give a unit price alongside the selling price (Article 5) and lists the permitted units in Schedule 1; it concerns consumer sales. **[U]** The page I fetched was only the table of contents, so the actual unit list (the extract suggested kilogram, litre, metre, square metre) is not confirmed. **[consumer]** *gov* — [The Price Marking Order 2004](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2004/102/contents/made)
- **[V]** GOV.UK VAT rates: standard 20% ("Most goods and services"), reduced 5% ("Some goods and services, for example, children's car seats and home energy"), zero 0%; the page I fetched did not state rates for construction or energy-saving materials. *gov* — [GOV.UK VAT rates](https://www.gov.uk/vat-rates)

**Basket optimisation complexity (ISOP literature)**
- **[V]** The Internet Shopping Optimization Problem (Blazewicz, Kovalyov, Musial, Urbanski, Wojciechowski, *peer-reviewed*, Int. J. Appl. Math. Comput. Sci. 2010) is a "multiple-item multiple-shop optimization problem" minimising total expense including delivery; the paper proves strong NP-hardness and gives polynomial algorithms for special cases. **[consumer]** — [Internet Shopping Optimization Problem](https://zbc.uz.zgora.pl/repozytorium/Content/46859/download)
- **[V]** Bulteau, Hermelin, Labarre and Vialette ("The Clever Shopper Problem", *preprint*) summarise the base problem as "NP-hard in the strong sense even when all products are free and all delivery costs are equal to one" with "no polynomial (c ln n)-approximation algorithm (for any 0 < c < 1) unless P = NP". For shops that give a discount once a purchase threshold is reached (exactly a free-delivery threshold): NP-complete "even when only two shops are available" (prices in binary), W[1]-hard in the number of shops even with unary prices, no polynomial kernel; polynomial if every shop sells at most two items; an FPT algorithm in the number of items n with running time O(m 3^n) by dynamic programming over subsets; an XP algorithm O(n m W^m) (W = sum of prices); FPT in the number of shops when all prices are equal. — [The Clever Shopper Problem](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1802.07944)
- **[V]** With price discounts and delivery costs (Musial et al., *peer-reviewed* Bull. Pol. Acad. Sci. Tech. Sci. 2016; earlier SCITEPRESS 2014 position paper): the problem "is NP-hard in the strong sense"; heuristics (including MinMin and a cellular algorithm) were tested on real data from 32 US web-stores and compared with a branch-and-bound optimum on small instances; the authors report solutions "close to the optimum in a reasonable time". **[consumer]** **[non-UK]** — [Algorithms solving the ISOP with price discounts](https://journals.pan.pl//Content/84537/PDF/10.1515bpasts-2016-0056.pdf); [Musial et al. 2014](https://www.scitepress.org/Papers/2014/51126/51126.pdf)

**Freshness**
- **[V]** Google Merchant Center: shoppers "expect to view the same price and availability on your landing page as they view in the ad or listing"; a mismatch produces a warning or "preemptive disapproval"; sellers should "ensure that updates to your site and product data happen at the same time" and, if prices change several times a day, use the Inventory API for faster updates. *docs* — [Google: price and availability mismatch troubleshooter](https://support.google.com/merchants/answer/7334523?hl=en)
- **[U]** A third-party blog snippet says "All products expire after 30 days" in Merchant Center unless data is refreshed; I could not confirm this on a Google help page (two Google pages I fetched did not mention expiry). *trade/ref* — [DataFeedWatch](https://www.datafeedwatch.com/blog/10-common-google-merchant-center-errors)

**Outlier detection and index checks**
- **[V]** ONS consumer-price validation (ESCoE technical report TR-07, 24 Jul 2020): phase one is a min-max range per item built from the previous month's valid quote and widened by item-group percentages, applied to both price level and price change; phase two is the Tukey algorithm: sort price relatives, take a trimmed mean after removing the top and bottom 5%, form upper and lower "midmeans", set limits at the trimmed mean plus/minus the distance to the midmeans, manually validate anything outside, and treat failures "within 10 index points of the item index" as valid. The same report says Eurostat refers to Van der Grient and de Haan (2010) for scanner-data filters: month-on-month changes "that exceed a factor of 4 are considered outliers", plus a dumping filter for steep price drops with collapsing sales; it also describes the CPI Manual's median/quartile-ratio limits with a minimum distance (for example five per cent). **[consumer]** *gov* — [ESCoE TR-07](https://escoe-website.s3.amazonaws.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/08/04130542/ESCoE-TR-07.pdf)
- **[V]** The construction material price indices (DBT, "Building Materials and Components Statistics") are index numbers, not prices: the page says they are "set at 100 at the base year of 2015", published monthly, with materials content "in the main, based on ONS PPIs" (it describes the trend of input costs "for example, factory gate prices charged by materials manufacturers"); the methodology page lists no specific materials and warns that "The indices do not take into account current market conditions experienced by a contractor ... purchasing from sub-contractors, merchants or factors (for example, materials discounts or premiums ...)". *gov* — [DBT CMPI methodology](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/building-materials-and-components-methodology/building-materials-and-components-statistics-material-price-indices-methodology)
- **[V]** The data.gov.uk record the Spec links ("indices of construction costs wages and material prices", MHCLG) describes UK construction material price indices "monthly; 2010 = 100", licence OGL, last updated 18 January 2017, and states these are indices rather than raw prices. *gov* — [data.gov.uk dataset](https://www.data.gov.uk/dataset/c3dca187-5285-40d6-9a3b-8b048bce49c4/indices-of-construction-costs-wages-and-material-prices)

**Source trust**
- **[V]** Truth discovery (survey, *preprint*): it "integrates multi-source noisy information by estimating the reliability of each source". **[non-product]** — [A Survey on Truth Discovery](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1505.02463)
- **[V]** Knowledge-Based Trust (Google, *preprint*): scores a web source by the correctness of the facts extracted from it, separating extraction errors from source errors, applied to 2.8B facts and 119M web pages. **[non-product]** — [Knowledge-Based Trust](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1502.03519)

### Inferences
- **Decision metric.** Compare options by landed cost of the required quantity, not by unit price. Unit price (per m2, m, kg, litre, each) is a screening and display normalisation; the optimiser needs, per (line, merchant, SKU), the cheapest way to cover the quantity: a small unbounded-covering-knapsack over the merchant's pack sizes, minimum order quantity and buy-in multiples (dynamic programming or enumeration over pack counts), with the surplus recorded as explicit waste. Over-buying is allowed when cheaper but must be shown.
- **VAT.** Store ex-VAT and inc-VAT amounts, the observed basis and the VAT rate per SKU (20% standard, but zero and reduced rates exist), convert only with a known rate, and compare on one basis chosen from the buyer's VAT status. A merchant's free-delivery threshold can be defined on a different basis from the displayed price, so treat the threshold basis as a per-merchant attribute. No source I found says which basis UK trade sites default to.
- **Delivery and landed cost.** Delivery is a step function of the merchant subtotal (fee below a threshold, possibly tiers, possibly a minimum order value, possibly pallet or surcharge rules); the price comparison must include it, which is what makes the multi-merchant problem combinatorial (section 8).
- **Staleness.** Model every price as an observation: value, basis, observed_at, source kind (merchant API/feed, merchant page, aggregator, user-entered) and optional validity. Use a time-to-live or hazard rate per source kind to classify fresh / stale / expired, let stale prices enter the optimiser only as "indicative" with an uncertainty margin, and require a fresh verified quote before any order (R1, R5). The numeric TTLs have to be measured; I found no UK evidence on change frequency.
- **Sanity checks.** Layered: (1) hard guards (non-positive, unit-price outside a category range, factor-of-4 month-on-month jump); (2) robust cross-merchant comparison on log price for the same product group (median and MAD or Tukey-style limits) once at least three sources exist; (3) ONS-style price-relative checks per merchant over time; (4) index ratio check: compare a product family's own price relative over a month against the matching CMPI series' relative, flagging large divergences. The CMPI is factory-gate, not merchant, and is an index, so it can validate direction and magnitude of change, never an absolute price level.
- **Spec pointer.** The data.gov.uk MHCLG dataset linked in the Spec appears stale (last updated 2017, base 2010 = 100); the live series is DBT's CMPI (base 2015 = 100), whose per-material coverage still has to be confirmed from the monthly data tables.
- **Source trust.** Start with priors by source kind and merchant, learn per-source reliability from agreement with later verified quotes (truth-discovery style), and use it to widen uncertainty margins or break ties rather than to hide conflicts. Never let a low-trust source silently win a basket.

### Gaps
- No UK builders'-merchant data on price-change frequency, delivery fee and threshold schedules, minimum order values, pack conventions, or trade-versus-retail VAT display; no source on pack-size rounding practice (the knapsack framing is my inference).
- No quantitative staleness model: the one scraped-price stickiness paper I tried to open (Cavallo, Review of Economics and Statistics, MIT Press page) returned HTTP 403, web search was then exhausted, and Google's 30-day expiry is only a third-party claim.
- All ISOP evidence is consumer, US book-shop or theoretical; I found no B2B or building-materials basket study.
- I could not confirm which materials the DBT CMPI series cover (the methodology page lists none; the statistics landing page I tried returned 404).

## 5. Evaluation: gold-set design, metrics with confidence bounds, cost per 1,000 lines, and shadow-mode rollout

### Takeaway
A 100-line gold set cannot certify the Spec's "below 0.5% wrong auto-accepts" target: zero errors in n auto-accepted lines only bounds the true rate at about 3/n (2.95% at n = 100, 4.13% at n = 71), and roughly 600 error-free auto-accepted decisions are needed to show below 0.5% with 95% one-sided confidence. Design the gold set like WDC Products (explicit corner-case, unseen-entity and size dimensions), report exact binomial bounds on the auto-accepted subset, choose the gate threshold with a risk-control procedure, audit production by sampling, and treat the human buyer's decision as the "production" side of a shadow test.

### Cited Findings
- **[V]** Spec targets: gold set of "100 real order lines minimum, including at least 20 deliberate non-matches"; the v1 prototype "scored 35/35 top-1 on 35 synthetic positives"; primary metric "wrong auto-accepts" with target "zero on the gold set, then below 0.5% in production"; v1 auto-accept rate 71% on synthetic data; gate thresholds 0.75 / 0.05 / 0.35; phases 1-4 start with shadow mode. — [Spec](/home/user/ai-commerce-ideas/docs/product/07-product-matching-engine-spec-v2.md)
- **[V]** WDC Products shows a gold-set recipe: vary corner-case share (80/50/20%), unseen-entity share (0/50/100%) and development-set size; hard cases are chosen by title-similarity mining; labels carry about 4% noise (two annotators 4.00% and 4.17%, kappa 0.91, from a 600-pair sample). **[consumer]** — [WDC Products paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2301.09521)
- **[V]** Amazon ESCI uses graded relevance (Exact, Substitute, Complement, Irrelevant), about 130 thousand queries and 2.6 million judgements. **[consumer]** — [ESCI](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2206.06588)
- **[V]** Blocking/retrieval is evaluated with Pairs Completeness (recall), Pairs Quality (precision) and Reduction Ratio. — [Blocking and Filtering survey](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1905.06167)
- **[V]** Rule of three: "if a certain event did not occur in a sample with n subjects, the interval from 0 to 3/n is a 95% confidence interval for the rate of occurrences"; multipliers 3.51, 4.61 and 5.3 give 97%, 99% and 99.5% intervals; the rule is credited to Hanley and Lippman-Hand, JAMA 1983. *trade/ref* — [Wikipedia: Rule of three](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rule_of_three_(statistics))
- **[V]** Selective prediction with a guaranteed risk (SGR) and Learn-then-Test both choose an abstention threshold from labelled calibration data so that a risk bound holds with stated probability: SGR relies on "the tightest possible numerical generalization bound for a single classifier, based on a test over a labeled sample", and Learn-then-Test says that for the binary loss of selective classification "we use the exact binomial tail bound". **[non-product]** — [Selective Classification for DNNs](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1705.08500); [Learn then Test](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.01052)
- **[V]** OASIS (*peer-reviewed*, PVLDB 2017) addresses "extreme class imbalance between matching and non-matching records" in entity-resolution evaluation with adaptive importance sampling and reports "labelling reductions of up to 83% without loss to estimate accuracy". **[consumer]** — [OASIS](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1703.00617)
- **[V]** An independent preprint found that a few-shot prompt revision that "looked effective on a small validation sample" "reduced full-scale performance to F1=0.914" on Abt-Buy (its zero-shot LLM scored 0.948), so "small sample prompt evaluation can be misleading". **[consumer]** — [When Do LLMs Actually Help With Data Quality?](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.18158)
- **[V]** Lin 2026 reports cost per error avoided relative to the fuzzy baseline: $0.0106 (Sonnet 4.6), $0.0035 (Haiku 4.5), $0.0003 (Gemini 2.5 Flash-Lite); costs came from API-reported tokens at list prices without batch or cache discounts. **[consumer]** — [Lin 2026 PDF](https://zenodo.org/records/20089436/files/Lin-LLM-Dedupe-Comparison-Study.pdf)
- **[V]** Anthropic list prices (USD per million input/output tokens, read 2026-10-06): Haiku 4.5 $1/$5; Sonnet 4.6 $3/$15; Sonnet 5.5 $2/$10; Opus 5.5 $4/$20. Batch API gives 50% off both directions; prompt-cache reads cost 0.1x base input (5-minute writes 1.25x); "Claude 4.7 and later models ... use a newer tokenizer ... approximately 30% more tokens for the same text" (the page says Claude Sonnet 4.6 and earlier models use the previous tokenizer and does not state Haiku 4.5's tokenizer). *docs* — [Anthropic pricing](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing)
- **[V]** Shadow testing, as defined by SageMaker: the platform "routes a copy of the inference requests" to the new variant, "Only the responses of the production variant are returned to the calling application", and shadow responses can be discarded or logged "for offline comparison". *docs* — [SageMaker shadow tests](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/sagemaker/latest/dg/shadow-tests.html)

### Inferences
- **Own computation [E]: exact (Clopper-Pearson) upper bounds on the true wrong-auto-accept rate when zero errors are observed in n auto-accepted lines**, one-sided 95% / two-sided 95%: n = 20 13.9% / 16.8%; 35 8.2% / 10.0%; 50 5.8% / 7.1%; 71 4.13% / 5.06%; 100 2.95% / 3.62%; 200 1.49% / 1.83%; 300 0.99% / 1.22%; 500 0.60% / 0.74%; 600 0.498% / 0.613%; 1,000 0.30% / 0.37%; 2,000 0.15% / 0.18%; 5,000 0.06% / 0.07%. Error-free sample sizes needed (n >= ln(alpha)/ln(1-p)): for 1.0% 299 / 368 / 459 (alpha 0.05 / 0.025 / 0.01); for 0.5% 598 / 736 / 919; for 0.2% 1,497 / 1,843 / 2,301; for 0.1% 2,995 / 3,688 / 4,603. With 1,000 auto-accepts, one wrong gives a 0.47% one-sided bound, two 0.63%, three 0.77%, five 1.05%. Wilson 95% interval for a top-3 recall of 95/100 is 0.888-0.978.
- Implication: the denominator that matters is auto-accepted decisions, not gold-set lines. On the Spec's own scenario (100 lines, 71% auto-accepted, zero wrong) the bound is 4.13%. "Zero wrong on the gold set" is a smoke test; "upper 95% bound below 0.5%" needs about 600 error-free auto-accepts (about 850 lines at a 71% auto-accept rate), which is a shadow-phase sizing target.
- Gold-set composition (proposal): (a) positives at three match levels (generic spec, brand-restricted, MPN) with set-valued truth (every qualifying SKU accepted, one preferred); (b) systematically generated hard negatives, one per catalogue SKU, differing in exactly one critical attribute (thickness, width/length, edge type, strength or class grade, pack size, finish), mirroring WDC's similarity-mined corner cases; (c) true non-matches (nothing suitable in the catalogue), 20-30%; (d) ambiguous lines whose correct outcome is "review" (quantity-versus-size, missing thickness, unit conflicts), scored on routing; (e) an unseen slice (new merchants or product families absent from the approval store); (f) double labelling of at least a quarter of lines to estimate noise and kappa. Report every metric per slice, not only in aggregate.
- Metrics: wrong auto-accept rate with exact bound (primary); auto-accept coverage and the risk-coverage curve, using an SGR or Learn-then-Test style calibration to pick the 0.75/0.05 gate values instead of fixing them by hand; retrieval recall at 50 (the Pairs Completeness analogue, target comparable to Sparkly's 98.7-100% at k = 50) and top-1/top-3 of the ranked list; review rate and review minutes per line; false-reject rate; LLM tokens and cost per 1,000 lines; cost per error avoided as a secondary comparison across judge models.
- **Own computation [E]: LLM judge cost per 1,000 order lines** with list prices above, assuming a judge call of 1,500 input and 200 output tokens (my assumption for the line, 5 candidates with attributes, 3 approved examples and instructions; Peeters/Bizer measured 942-1,009 prompt tokens for 10-shot pair prompts) and escalation share e (fraction of lines that reach the judge). Per 1,000 judge calls: Haiku 4.5 $2.50, Sonnet 5.5 $5.00, Sonnet 4.6 $7.50, Opus 5.5 $10.00. Per 1,000 lines: Haiku 4.5 $0.25 / $0.75 / $2.50 at e = 10% / 30% / 100%; Sonnet 5.5 $0.50 / $1.50 / $5.00; Sonnet 4.6 $0.75 / $2.25 / $7.50; Opus 5.5 $1.00 / $3.00 / $10.00. At e = 30% the Batch API halves these (Haiku $0.375); caching 1,000 of the 1,500 input tokens at 0.1x lowers Haiku at e = 30% from $0.75 to $0.48. With larger prompts (3,000 in / 300 out) Haiku at e = 30% is $1.35. Maximum affordable escalation share = budget / (cost per 1,000 judge calls); for a USD 2.00 budget that is about 80% (Haiku), 40% (Sonnet 5.5), 27% (Sonnet 4.6), 20% (Opus 5.5). The Spec's ceiling is in pounds and I did not source an exchange rate; Sonnet 5.5's newer tokenizer may inflate token counts by about 30% relative to the older one, which these figures do not include.
- Shadow rollout: map the Spec's Phase 1 onto shadow testing, with the human buyer's choice as the production path. Log the parse, retrieved ids and scores, validator results, both permuted judge outputs, the gate decision, the human choice and time to decision. Guard against anchoring (if reviewers see suggestions, agreement overstates accuracy: hide suggestions on a random slice or add blind second review), against leakage (approvals become few-shot examples, so evaluate on a time- or signature-split), and for production use a stratified or importance-sampled audit of auto-accepted lines (OASIS-style) so the wrong-accept rate is monitored with fewer labels. Re-run the Spec's one-off WDC check only as a sanity test because WDC labels have about 4% noise.

### Gaps
- No evidence on the real mix of generic, branded and MPN lines, on inter-annotator agreement for construction order lines, or on automation bias in procurement review (not searched).
- No published gold-set protocol for B2B purchasing or construction product matching; the WDC and ESCI designs are consumer.
- Exchange-rate conversion of the Spec's GBP budget, and real token counts for the judge prompt, still have to be measured on the actual prompt and tokenizer.

## 6. Key question 1: which concrete algorithm choices for each pipeline step under the offline, no-heavy-dependency constraint, and what is the production upgrade path?

### Takeaway
Implement parse, attribute validation, re-validation and the gate as deterministic typed code with no third-party dependency; implement retrieval as pure-Python BM25 over words, character 3-grams and canonical numeric/grade tokens fused with a hashed character-n-gram cosine by one tuned weight; keep embeddings and the LLM behind ports with deterministic fakes. Upgrade to Postgres (pg_trgm, a BM25 extension, pgvector), a small open embedding model, a fine-tuned cross-encoder and a Haiku-class judge only when gold-set recall, review-rate or cost metrics justify each step.

### Cited Findings
- **[V]** Library facts that drive the dependency choices: Pint and quantulum3 failed on the trade patterns tested (section 2 [E]); quantulum3's wheel is about 10.6 MB with an optional GloVe classifier; RapidFuzz is MIT with a pure-Python fallback; bm25s and rank-bm25 need only numpy; sentence-transformers pulls in torch and transformers; hnswlib is source-only on PyPI; Python's built-in string hash is randomised per process. — [quantulum3](https://pypi.org/project/quantulum3/); [RapidFuzz](https://pypi.org/project/rapidfuzz/); [bm25s](https://pypi.org/project/bm25s/); [sentence-transformers](https://pypi.org/project/sentence-transformers/); [hnswlib](https://pypi.org/project/hnswlib/); [Python docs](https://docs.python.org/3/using/cmdline.html)
- **[V]** Retrieval evidence: Sparkly's top-k BM25 over 3-grams reached recall 98.7-100% at k = 50 on 15 datasets; BEIR shows BM25 is a robust zero-shot baseline; dense retrievers lose on entity-centric lookups (49.7% vs 72.0%); embedding models are near chance on numeric differences (average 0.54, MiniLM-L6 50.6); convex-combination fusion beat RRF in the Bruch et al. analysis while RRF (k = 60) needs no tuning. — [Sparkly](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol16/p1507-paulsen.pdf); [BEIR](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2104.08663); [Sciavolino et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2109.08535); [Numeracy Gap](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.05691); [Bruch et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2210.11934); [RRF](https://plg.uwaterloo.ca/~gvcormac/cormacksigir09-rrf.pdf)
- **[V]** Postgres facts: pg_trgm ignores non-alphanumerics, default similarity threshold 0.3, KNN ordering only via GiST; native `ts_rank` uses no global statistics; pgvector filters after approximate index scans (iterative scans from 0.8.0); pg_textsearch provides BM25 under the PostgreSQL licence; ParadeDB's community edition is AGPL-3.0. — [pg_trgm](https://www.postgresql.org/docs/current/pgtrgm.html); [text search ranking](https://www.postgresql.org/docs/current/textsearch-controls.html); [pgvector](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/pgvector/pgvector/master/README.md); [pg_textsearch](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/timescale/pg_textsearch/main/README.md); [ParadeDB](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/paradedb/paradedb/main/LICENSE)
- **[V]** Judge evidence: Haiku 4.5 F1 0.958 vs Sonnet 4.6 0.961 on 2,518 ambiguous WDC pairs at $1.30 vs $3.95 per 1,000 pairs (single preprint); small-model results swing with prompt and few-shot choices (GPT-4o-mini 16.85-81.15 F1 across prompts; 81.15 zero-shot vs 67.48 six-shot); selecting from a candidate list beats pairwise matching on average but suffers position bias; permuting option contents is the debiasing approach. — [Lin 2026](https://zenodo.org/records/20089436); [Peeters et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.11244); [ComEM](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2405.16884); [Zheng et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2309.03882)
- **[V]** Gate evidence: SGR and Learn-then-Test give probabilistic risk guarantees for a confidence threshold using binomial tail bounds; verbalised LLM confidence had ECE 0.062-0.346 and is "useful as a routing signal but imperfectly calibrated". — [SGR](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1705.08500); [LTT](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.01052); [Lin 2026 PDF](https://zenodo.org/records/20089436/files/Lin-LLM-Dedupe-Comparison-Study.pdf)
- **[V]** Learning evidence: for supervised pairwise matching "a medium-scale cross-encoder is a strong default"; fine-tuning cost "can be amortized after a few thousand API calls"; same-title negatives in training/test mismatch caused large precision losses; AnyMatch reached within 4.4% of GPT-4-based matching at 3,899 times lower inference cost. — [Beyond Scale and Generation](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.24688); [AnyMatch](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.04073)

### Inferences
Recommended choices (offline = unit tests and the default build; production = upgrade when the stated trigger is met):

| Step | Offline / pure-Python implementation | Production upgrade | Trigger to upgrade |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 Parse | Hand-written tokenizer and grammar to typed candidates (`Decimal` value, unit, role: thickness/width/length/diameter/quantity/pack/grade). Emit every plausible reading; "N x size" and metre-pair/mm-chain conflicts flagged ambiguous (routes to review). Units, synonyms and size-equivalence tables in reviewed data files. No Pint/quantulum3 at runtime. | Same parser; LLM-drafted glossary and catalogue attribute extraction run through the same parser as a validator (values must occur in source text, per Spec). | Parser failure rate on gold lines (unparsed or ambiguous share) above an agreed level. |

| 5 Re-validate | Same comparator as step 3 applied to the judge's choice. | Unchanged. | n/a |
| 6 Gate | Deterministic function of top score, lead, failed checks, judge-scorer agreement, signature approvals; thresholds read from the resolved profile, never hard-coded. | Choose thresholds with an SGR or Learn-then-Test style binomial-bound calibration on labelled data; set auto-accept only when the upper bound on wrong-accept is below target. | At least about 600 error-free auto-accepts for a 0.5% bound (section 5). |
| 7 Learn | Append-only approvals store keyed by normalised signature; nearest-approval retrieval by the same BM25. | Train a small cross-encoder reranker on approvals including systematic sibling negatives (22.7M-parameter MiniLM class, Apache-2.0); consider a fine-tuned small LM (AnyMatch-style) later; balance same-title negatives across train and test splits. | A few thousand labelled approvals, or judge spend above budget. |

- Why this split: the evidence says numbers, grades and packs are exactly where lexical, embedding and LLM scorers are least reliable (section 3 [E] table; Numeracy Gap; Lin FP counts), so the deterministic comparator must be the last word, while the statistical components are only for recall and ranking. Everything on the offline side is a pure function of its inputs, which makes the tests deterministic.
- Cost control: skip the judge when the signature is already approved, when scorer and validators agree with a clear lead (an independent preprint found rules matching zero-shot LLM F1 on Abt-Buy), and cache the stable prompt prefix; only about e = 10-30% of lines should need the judge for the Spec's budget to hold with Haiku-class pricing (section 5 [E]).
- Statistical gate: because the target is a wrong-accept rate, calibrate the threshold on a labelled calibration set with an exact binomial upper bound, re-estimate after any catalogue, prompt or model change, and treat conformal prediction sets as a review aid (show the set of plausible SKUs) rather than as the gate itself, since marginal coverage guarantees do not bound a conditional error rate for the auto-accepted subset.

### Gaps
- All upgrade triggers are proposals; none is backed by a UK data measurement. Embedding models were not benchmarked on trade titles, and judge prompts were not tested on size/grade siblings.
- Whether the team's Postgres host supports pgvector or a BM25 extension was not checked.
- No source on how often catalogue titles omit critical attributes (which drives the unknown-attribute policy).

## 7. Key question 2: what does the best evidence say about LLM-judge reliability on near-duplicate product pairs with size or grade differences?

### Takeaway
No study isolates this case, and none uses construction products. The nearest evidence says strong LLMs are good on ambiguous consumer pairs (F1 about 0.96) but not safe on their own: the best model still accepted about 0.8% of ambiguous non-matches even with a prompt that warned about size and model differences, results swing by tens of F1 points with prompt and demonstration choice, cheaper models degrade under few-shot examples, confidence is poorly calibrated, and published LLM error classes ("ignoring minor but significant differences", over-weighting brand and model number, variant misreads) are exactly size/grade confusion.

### Cited Findings
- **[V]** WDC Products corner cases (near-duplicate non-matches, similar-looking matches) are where precision breaks: medium development set, 80% corner cases, seen products: RoBERTa precision 66.61 vs recall 79.07, Ditto 70.97 vs 77.53; hosted LLMs on the same 80% corner-case benchmark scored F1 89.61 (GPT-4), 87.64 (GPT-4o), 81.15 (GPT-4o-mini) with best prompts. **[consumer]** — [WDC Products paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2301.09521); [Peeters et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.11244)
- **[V]** LLM error classes for false positives on Walmart-Amazon (derived by GPT-4 from its own explanations): overemphasis on matching attributes such as brand or model number, ignoring minor but significant differences in product type, model or features, misreading accessory/variant information, overlooked price discrepancy, condition or quality differences. **[consumer]** — [Peeters et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.11244)
- **[V]** Lin 2026 on 2,518 ambiguous WDC pairs (1,719 non-matches): false positives Sonnet 4.6 14, Haiku 4.5 15, Gemini 2.5 Flash-Lite 32; precision 0.982 / 0.980 / 0.953; the prompt explicitly warned that "differences in size, model number, specifications, color, quantity, bundle composition, or product variant could indicate distinct products"; with confidence at least 0.95, Haiku's precision was 1.000 but recall only 0.447; verbalised-confidence ECE 0.062 / 0.088 / 0.346; Opus tied Haiku in the pilot. Single prompt, one run, no peer review. **[consumer]** — [Lin 2026 PDF](https://zenodo.org/records/20089436/files/Lin-LLM-Dedupe-Comparison-Study.pdf)
- **[V]** Model and prompt dependence: across ten prompts GPT-4o-mini F1 on WDC ranged 16.85-81.15, GPT-4o 21.91-87.64 and GPT-4 77.39-89.61; six-shot demonstrations lowered GPT-4o-mini from 81.15 to 67.48 and GPT-4 from 89.61 to 87.23 but raised GPT-4o from 87.64 to 89.96. **[consumer]** — [Peeters et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.11244)
- **[V]** Position bias in pick-one-of-N formats (ICLR 2024; ComEM): gold answer moved to option D cut gpt-3.5-turbo from 67.2 to 60.9; selecting accuracy "varies significantly as the position of the matching record increases". **[non-product]** for the first, **[consumer]** for the second. — [Zheng et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2309.03882); [ComEM](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2405.16884)
- **[V]** Re-sampling is not an uncertainty signal for a fixed prompt: 99.7% self-agreement over 5 runs at temperature 0.7, and majority voting added 0.005 F1 at 5x the cost; a prompt that looked better on a small sample was worse at scale. **[consumer]** — [When Do LLMs Actually Help With Data Quality?](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.18158)
- **[V]** Embedding-stage numeric blindness (so numeric differences must be caught after retrieval): 13 embedding models averaged 0.54 accuracy against 0.5 chance on numeric retrieval. **[non-product]** — [Revealing the Numeracy Gap](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.05691)
- **[V]** Shortcut learning when near-duplicates (same title) are rare in training but common in test: cross-encoder precision fell from 96.9 to 76.4 as model size grew from 0.6B to 8B (movie data). **[non-product]** — [Beyond Scale and Generation](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.24688)
- **[V]** Complementary failure modes: rule-based matching over-matches while LLMs fail on cross-script transliteration (sanctions data). **[non-product]** — [OpenSanctions Pairs](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.11051)

### Inferences
- Arithmetic on Lin's counts [E]: false-positive rate on ambiguous non-matches = 14/1,719 = 0.81% (Sonnet), 15/1,719 = 0.87% (Haiku), 32/1,719 = 1.86% (Flash-Lite); about 1.8-2.0% of the pairs the two Claude models called matches were wrong (Sonnet about 14 of ~766, Haiku about 15 of ~764). Those are floors for a hard consumer slice with a conservative prompt, not forecasts for plasterboard; the WDC "ambiguous" pairs are also not size-only siblings.
- Size and grade siblings differ from WDC hard negatives in one important way: the sibling differs in one number or code, so the textual similarity is near-maximal (section 3 [E] table: 0.76-0.97 on all lexical metrics). A judge that relies on holistic similarity will be at its weakest there, matching the "ignoring minor but significant differences" error class.
- The reliable design is to take the decision away from the LLM: use it to propose, rank and extract (attribute values with source spans, brand and synonym equivalence, unit semantics), and let the deterministic comparator decide equality on size, grade, class and pack, as the Spec's re-validation already does. Judge output that conflicts with the comparator becomes "review".
- Pick-of-5 judging should use two candidate orderings and require agreement; do not use temperature-sampling votes or verbalised confidence as the auto-accept criterion.
- Measure it directly before trusting any judge on materials: build a sibling-perturbation test set from the catalogue (for each SKU, lines that name it and sibling SKUs that differ in exactly one critical attribute), run each candidate model and prompt with and without few-shot examples and under both orderings, and report false-accept rate with an exact binomial bound. To show a sibling false-accept rate below 0.5% (95% one-sided) with zero errors needs at least 598 sibling trials (section 5 [E]).
- Skip the judge when validators and scorer already agree: an independent preprint found a simple rule baseline (F1 0.950) level with zero-shot LLM (0.948) when strong lexical signals exist, which both saves cost and removes the judge's failure surface on easy lines.

### Gaps
- No direct evidence on LLM-judge accuracy for numeric or grade-only differences in construction, MRO or any B2B catalogue; no UK data; every figure above is consumer or non-product.
- Lin 2026 (the Spec's cited source) is a single-author Zenodo preprint with one prompt and one run per model; the pilot comparisons (Opus, other vendors) are only summarised, not independently verified.
- LLM-MSMA (the Spec's construction-domain source) could not be read (HTTP 403).
- I did not test any model myself; the judge's behaviour on the team's lines remains unmeasured.

## 8. Key question 3: which basket-optimisation formulation is right for 5-15 lines, 3-6 merchants and delivery thresholds?

### Takeaway
Model it as "assign each line to one merchant, pay line costs plus a delivery fee that disappears once the merchant's subtotal reaches its free-delivery threshold". At 5-15 lines and 3-6 merchants this is exactly solvable: a pure-Python subset dynamic programme (O(m 3^n)) is a deterministic offline oracle (about 6 s at n = 15, m = 6 in my test) and a small MILP is the production solver (milliseconds in my tests); heuristics are not needed at this scale even though the general problem is strongly NP-hard.

### Cited Findings
- **[V]** The Internet Shopping Optimization Problem is strongly NP-hard (Blazewicz et al., *peer-reviewed*, 2010); it "admits no polynomial (c ln n)-approximation algorithm ... unless P = NP" (as summarised by Bulteau et al.). **[consumer]** — [ISOP](https://zbc.uz.zgora.pl/repozytorium/Content/46859/download); [The Clever Shopper Problem](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1802.07944)
- **[V]** For shops with a discount once a threshold is reached, the Clever Shopper problem is NP-complete even with two shops (prices in binary), W[1]-hard in the number of shops with unary prices, has an FPT algorithm in the number of items n with running time O(m 3^n) (dynamic programming over subsets: p_{<=j}(B') = min over B'' subset of B' of p_j(B'') + p_{<=j-1}(B' minus B'') where p_j(S) is the price of buying S at shop j with the discount included), is polynomial if each shop sells at most two items, and "generalises well-studied problems such as bin covering". — [The Clever Shopper Problem](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1802.07944)
- **[V]** Algorithms for ISOP with delivery costs and price discounts (*peer-reviewed*, 2016): strongly NP-hard; a branch-and-bound algorithm produced optima for small instances; heuristics were "close to the optimum" and scaled well on data from 32 US web-stores. **[consumer]** **[non-UK]** — [Musial et al. 2016](https://journals.pan.pl//Content/84537/PDF/10.1515bpasts-2016-0056.pdf)
- **[V]** Solver licences and footprints: HiGHS Python bindings highspy 1.15.1 MIT (numpy); SciPy 1.18.1 BSD; OR-tools 9.15 Apache-2.0 (numpy, pandas, protobuf); PuLP 4.0.0 MIT. *docs* — [highspy](https://pypi.org/project/highspy/); [SciPy](https://pypi.org/project/scipy/); [OR-tools](https://pypi.org/project/ortools/); [PuLP](https://pypi.org/project/PuLP/)

### Inferences
- **Formulation (mine).** Let c_ij be the landed cost of line i at merchant j after pack rounding (cheapest pack cover of the required quantity, on one VAT basis; infinite if j does not stock i), f_j the delivery fee below the threshold, T_j the free-delivery threshold. Variables: x_ij = 1 if line i is bought from j; y_j = 1 if merchant j is used; z_j = 1 if j's threshold is met.
  Minimise sum_ij c_ij x_ij + sum_j f_j (y_j - z_j), subject to sum_j x_ij = 1 for every line; x_ij <= y_j; sum_i c_ij x_ij >= T_j z_j; z_j <= y_j; all variables binary.
  Pack rounding stays outside the optimiser (it is per line and per merchant), which keeps the model linear. Natural extensions: delivery tiers (one binary per tier), minimum order value (sum_i c_ij x_ij >= M_j y_j), a cap on the number of deliveries (sum_j y_j <= K), stock or lead-time exclusions (drop c_ij), and splitting a line across merchants (integer pack variables, which the subset DP below does not cover).
- **Exact offline oracle (pure Python).** Per merchant j precompute g_j(S) for every subset S of lines: infinity if any line in S is unavailable, 0 for the empty set, otherwise the subtotal plus the fee when the subtotal is below T_j. Then F_1(S) = g_1(S) and F_j(S) = min over T subset of S of g_j(T) + F_{j-1}(S minus T), iterating submasks with `T = (T - 1) & S`; the answer is F_m(all lines), with argmins stored to recover the assignment. This is the Bulteau et al. O(m 3^n) algorithm, and it accepts any per-merchant cost function of the subset (fees, tiers, minimum orders, exclusions).
- **Own measurements [E]** (synthetic random instances: 40 "easy" and 40 "hard" with small price spread and large fees/thresholds; n 6-11, m 3-6): the DP and the MILP above (SciPy `optimize.milp`) returned identical optimal costs on all 80 instances, and DP, brute force and branch-and-bound agreed on five n = 7, m = 4 instances. Pure-Python DP time at m = 6: n = 12 0.18 s; n = 14 about 2.0 s; n = 15 5.7-6.5 s (m = 5: n = 12 0.15 s). A simple branch-and-bound with a weak bound (sum of per-line minimum prices) took 0.00-0.49 s on easy and some hard instances but 3.2 s at n = 12 and 22.0 s with 14.0M nodes at n = 14 on hard instances, so it is instance-dependent and unsafe as the only method. MILP times: 0.007-0.038 s for n <= 15, m <= 6; 0.011 s (n = 20, m = 6), 0.015 s (n = 30, m = 8), 0.024 s (n = 50, m = 8).
- **Recommendation.** For 5-15 lines and 3-6 merchants use the exact formulation: DP in the test suite and as a dependency-free fallback; MILP (HiGHS via SciPy, OR-tools or PuLP) in production, cross-checked against the DP on random instances in CI. Heuristics from the ISOP literature (greedy cheapest-per-line followed by consolidation to cross thresholds, MinMin) are only a contingency for baskets far beyond 50 lines or when no solver is installable. Present the approver with a small set of distinct baskets (cheapest overall, fewest deliveries, single merchant) with savings versus the single-merchant basket, since nothing is ordered without approval (R1). Keep money as Decimal at the boundaries and integer pence inside solvers (R5), and recompute with fresh quotes before approval because thresholds make the optimum sensitive to small price changes.
- Because z_j is only credited when the subtotal truly reaches T_j, price staleness near a threshold can flip the optimum; for baskets whose optimum sits within a small margin of a threshold, show the margin to the buyer.

### Gaps
- All timings are mine, on synthetic instances with random prices; real merchant data (many SKUs per line, pack options, multi-tier delivery tables, minimum order values, excluded items from threshold calculation) was not available, and worst-case adversarial instances were not tested.
- The ISOP literature I read is theoretical or US consumer book-shop data; I did not find a study of building-materials baskets or of B2B delivery-fee structures.
- I did not read the discount-function variants in depth (concave price breaks, per-item quantity discounts); they would extend the model beyond fee-and-threshold.
- SciPy's `milp` being HiGHS-based is from my prior knowledge and the packages' PyPI metadata, not a documentation page I re-read this session.
