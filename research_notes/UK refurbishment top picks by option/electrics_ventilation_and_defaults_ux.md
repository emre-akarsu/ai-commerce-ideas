## (A) Which bathroom electrics and ventilation options are budget / most_used / premium in the UK (2024-2026), and what should the defaults be?

### Takeaway
A 100 mm axial wall/ceiling fan with an overrun timer (optionally humidistat) is the mass-market default and meets Approved Document F's 15 l/s intermittent rate; continuous-running dMEV (8 l/s) and inline fans are the premium/quiet routes. Defaults recommended: 100 mm axial fan with humidistat + timer, rigid duct with ≤1.5 m flexible final connection, 3-4 fire-rated IP65 LED downlights, 2-gang dual-voltage shaver socket (or omit if the LED mirror has one), 500 x 800 mm chrome ladder towel rail plumbed to the heating (dual-fuel as upgrade), LED demister mirror as optional, electric UFH mat optional/off by default, and a basic chrome towel ring + toilet-roll holder.

### Cited Findings

**Regulatory baseline (ventilation)**
- Approved Document F (2021 edition, Vol. 1 Dwellings, in force 15 June 2022) requires bathrooms with a bath or shower to have 15 l/s intermittent extract or 8 l/s continuous extract. (Primary ADF PDF not fetched this session; rates are the widely-reported values and match my prior knowledge.)
- Ducting practice: flexible duct should be used only as a final connection, not exceed 1,500 mm, and be pulled taut to at least 90% of its length; rigid duct for the main run; support at ≤600 mm centres; joints mechanically secured and sealed. [practitioner/warranty provider] — [LABC Warranty technical blog](https://www.labcwarranty.co.uk/technical-blog/insulating-extraction-ducts); combined rigid main run + short flexible connections described as the most common residential set-up [secondary/blog] — [Aircondlounge](https://aircondlounge.com/what-type-of-duct-to-use-for-a-bathroom-ventilation-fan/).

### Inferences
- Default fan = 100 mm axial with humidistat + overrun timer (CQF100HT-class, ~£45-50): meets ADF's intermittent 15 l/s comfortably (75 m³/h ≈ 21 l/s free-air; installed performance is lower once ducted, so short duct runs matter), and the humidistat removes reliance on occupants. Budget = timer-only (~£23); premium = quiet continuous dMEV (~£100-150) or inline (~£110-200) where long duct runs or noise matter. The 75 m³/h → l/s conversion is my arithmetic; installed flow depends on duct resistance.
- Default ducting = rigid 100 mm main run with ≤1.5 m taut flexible connection; offer "all-flexible" only as a non-default with a warning, since guidance limits flex length.
- Default lighting = 3-4 fire-rated IP65 downlights at ~£6-9 each (fire rating matters where the ceiling has a room/loft above; IP65 covers zone 1). Ceiling fitting is a lower-cost alternative but was not priced.
- Shaver socket is redundant if the chosen LED mirror has one; the wizard should de-duplicate (mirror with socket → no separate socket by default).
- Towel rail default = plumbed 500 x 800 chrome ladder (~£40-65); dual-fuel kit (+£35-65) as the "better" upgrade for summer use; electric-only for rooms without a convenient heating circuit.
- UFH should default off (optional) because it adds an electrician + thermostat + floor build-up cost; offer it when the floor is being re-tiled.
- Electrical work in bathrooms is typically notifiable in England (Part P), so electrics options imply an electrician; this is from prior knowledge, not verified this session.

## (B) What does UX and decision-science evidence say about a wizard with few questions, pre-selected defaults and tiered options, and what are the ethical limits?

### Takeaway
Defaults are among the most reliable choice-architecture effects (meta-analytic d ≈ 0.63-0.68; CMA: a pre-selected default is on average 27% more likely to be chosen out of two options), and in configurators the default level acts as a reference point. Choice overload is real only under specific conditions (complex sets, hard tasks, uncertain preferences, effort minimisation), which fits a home-refurb wizard. So: short linear wizard, 3 tiered options per question, a pre-selected default that is the honest best fit (not the most expensive), progressive disclosure for extras, and easy change. UK regulators treat defaults as acceptable when they serve consumers but flag harmful pre-selection, drip pricing and decoys.

### Cited Findings

**Default effect**
- [peer-reviewed] Meta-analysis of 58 default studies (N = 73,675): defaults shift choices by d = 0.68 (95% CI 0.53-0.83); stronger in consumer domains, weaker in environmental; work via endorsement and endowment; a few studies found no or negative effects. — [Jachimowicz, Duncan, Weber & Johnson 2019, Behavioural Public Policy 3(2):159-186](https://ideas.repec.org/a/cup/bpubpo/v3y2019i02p159-186_00.html); [Columbia press release](https://business.columbia.edu/press-release/cbs-press-releases/defaults-are-not-same-default)
- [regulator] CMA (5 April 2022): "Defaults are one of the strongest and most reliable practices that influence consumer behaviour"; its analysis of 58 academic studies found "a pre-selected default option is on average 27% more likely to be selected out of two options than if there were no default option"; mechanisms are effort, implied endorsement and endowment/reference point (para 4.27-4.28). — [CMA discussion paper PDF](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/624c27c68fa8f527710aaf58/Online_choice_architecture_discussion_paper.pdf)
- [peer-reviewed, not fetched this session] Johnson & Goldstein (2003, Science 302:1338-1339), "Do defaults save lives?": opt-out countries show far higher organ-donor consent than opt-in countries. — [Science DOI 10.1126/science.1091721](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1091721) (cited from prior knowledge; verify figures before quoting).
- [peer-reviewed] Default bias in product configurators: in a simulated racing-bike configurator, moving a feature's default from lowest to highest level raised sales; the default acts as a reference point lifting nearby levels; to maximise sales the default "should be set at a level between the medium and highest price level". — [Herrmann, Goldstein, Stadler, Landwehr, Heitmann, Hofstetter 2011, J. Retailing & Consumer Services 18:483-491 (USI record)](https://search.usi.ch/en/publications/6899); [Univ. St Gallen record](https://www.alexandria.unisg.ch/208988/)
- [peer-reviewed/conference] Defaults are chosen more when the configuration starts with many options (depleting resources); ordering steps from many-to-few options lowered perceived difficulty and raised satisfaction. — [search snippet attributed to configurator research, Redalyc PDF](https://www.redalyc.org/pdf/965/96512484005.pdf) (closely mirrors Levav, Heitmann, Herrmann & Iyengar 2010, J. Political Economy, which I did not fetch; treat as indicative).
- [government] DBT research (published 8 Nov 2024): across 558 consumer journeys, defaults appeared on 49% of sampled sites/apps (412 instances); 64% of observed defaults steered to cheaper or similarly priced options; but in a choice experiment (n = 5,889) pre-selected defaults made consumers 60-70% more likely to select the more expensive option; "mimic" pre-selection had no significant effect. — [GOV.UK: prevalence and potential harm of defaults in online shopping](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/the-prevalence-and-potential-harm-of-defaults-in-online-shopping); [full report PDF](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/672208443aa14203d06ef49d/research-into-potentially-harmful-online-choice-architecture.pdf)

**Good-better-best tiering, decoys and compromise**
- [peer-reviewed] Adding an asymmetrically dominated alternative (decoy) raises the share of the option that dominates it, violating "regularity". — [Huber, Payne & Puto 1982, J. Consumer Research (2014 retrospective PDF)](https://people.duke.edu/~jch8/bio/Papers/HuberPaynePutoJMR%202014.pdf)
- [peer-reviewed] Compromise effect: an option becomes more likely to be chosen when it is the intermediate option, as people seek justifiable choices. — [Simonson 1989, J. Consumer Research (PDF)](https://cognition.aau.at/bg/BA/Simonson%20%281989%29.pdf); [Frontiers in Psychology 2022 review](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.869551/full)
- [regulator] CMA lists "choice overload and decoys" among practices the academic literature suggests are almost always harmful (marked *), alongside drip pricing, sludge, forced outcomes and complex language; defaults, ranking, framing and personalisation are harmful only in some circumstances. — [CMA discussion paper, Table 1](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/624c27c68fa8f527710aaf58/Online_choice_architecture_discussion_paper.pdf)

**Choice overload and option count**
- [peer-reviewed] Iyengar & Lepper (2000) jam study: 24-jar display attracted 60% of passers-by vs 40% for 6 jars, but ~3% of those stopping at 24 bought vs ~30% at 6. — [secondary summary, Virginia Postrel](https://vpostrel.com/articles/indecision-making); original: J. Personality & Social Psychology 79(6):995-1006 (not fetched).
- [peer-reviewed] Scheibehenne, Greifeneder & Todd (2010) meta-analysis: 63 conditions from 50 experiments (N = 5,036), mean effect size virtually zero with large heterogeneity. — [J. Consumer Research 37(3):409-425, IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/jconrs/v37y2010i3p409-425.html); [author PDF](https://abcwest.cogs.indiana.edu/pmwiki/pdf/scheibehenne.options.2010.pdf)
- [peer-reviewed] Chernev, Böckenholt & Goodman (2015, J. Consumer Psychology) meta-analysis (~99 effects): overload reliably appears with high choice-set complexity, high task difficulty, high preference uncertainty, and an effort-minimising goal. — [Kellogg research page](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/faculty/research/detail/2015/when-product-assortment-leads-to-choice-overload-a-conceptual); [Kellogg Insight](https://insight.kellogg.northwestern.edu/article/what-predicts-consumer-choice-overload). The "d ≈ 0.5-0.6 when all moderators high" figure came from a search summary, not the paper; verify before quoting.
- [secondary/blog, weak] "Limit primary options to 3-5 per decision point; mark one as recommended" — [atticusli.com](https://atticusli.com/blog/posts/paradox-of-choice-digital-products/). No peer-reviewed source found that establishes 3 as the optimum.

**Wizard / configurator UX (practitioner)**
- [practitioner] NN/g: use wizards "for novice users or infrequent processes (e.g., configuration or setup)"; show a step list and current step; enforce order; descriptive button labels; allow exit and resume with saved state; help beside, not over, the wizard; reuse previous selections as defaults. — [Budiu, NN/g 2017, "Wizards: Definition and Design Recommendations"](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/wizards/)
- [practitioner] NN/g progressive disclosure: "Initially, show users only a few of the most important options. Offer a larger set of specialized options upon request"; designs beyond 2 disclosure levels "typically have low usability". — [Nielsen 2006](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/progressive-disclosure/)
- [practitioner/platform guidance] Microsoft wizard guidelines: consumer wizards should be as short as possible with "pre-selected defaults for as many options as possible"; wizards supplement, not replace, direct task completion. — [Microsoft Learn, Wizards](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/previous-versions/ms997609(v=msdn.10))

**Ethics / UK regulation**
- [regulator] CMA: defaults "can be extremely valuable and can help consumers make good choices with minimal or no effort", but can lead to "buying unsuitable products" and reduce shopping around; harm grows when combined with sludge (hard to change the default), forced outcomes, framing a non-default as risky, or "messengers" presenting acceptance as the norm (paras 4.29-4.30). Without defaults "designed to be easily changed", consumers could be overwhelmed by active choices (para 11). — [CMA discussion paper](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/624c27c68fa8f527710aaf58/Online_choice_architecture_discussion_paper.pdf)
- [regulator] Remedies discussed include specifying consumer-interest defaults (e.g. UK GDPR ban on pre-ticked consent boxes) and requiring active choice (para 4.31-4.32). — same source.
- [peer-reviewed, cited by CMA] Smith, Goldstein & Johnson (2013), "Choice without awareness: ethical and policy implications of defaults", J. Public Policy & Marketing 32(2):159-172 — referenced in the CMA paper (not read directly). — [CMA discussion paper references](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/624c27c68fa8f527710aaf58/Online_choice_architecture_discussion_paper.pdf)

### Inferences
- A bathroom wizard fits the conditions where overload bites (unfamiliar product, uncertain preferences, effort-minimising user), so cutting each question to 3 options (budget / most_used / premium) with one pre-selected is well supported in direction, though "3" itself is convention plus the compromise effect, not a proven optimum.
- Given Herrmann et al. and the DBT experiment, pre-selecting premium would likely raise spend, which is exactly the harm the CMA/DBT describe. Ethical default = most_used / regulation-compliant option (e.g. humidistat+timer fan), with the reason shown ("meets Approved Document F; most common choice") and one-click change.
- Avoid: decoy tiers built only to push the middle option, pre-ticked paid add-ons (UFH, dual-fuel kit), hidden install costs (drip pricing), fake "popular" badges. Show the total price including VAT from the start, and label "most used" only where there is evidence.
- Keep progressive disclosure to one level ("Show more options"), keep linear steps with a progress indicator, and save state so users can leave and resume.

- No peer-reviewed study was found giving an optimal number of options per question; "3" is not empirically established here.
- CMA 2022 paper did not give specific guidance on tiered good-better-best pricing; CMA's later DMCC Act 2024 consumer-protection guidance was not reviewed.
