# Default the kit, ask three questions

A UK bathroom refurbishment kit can be pre-filled almost entirely from defaults. The evidence supports a "most_used" option for nearly every parameter, and the choices that really vary from job to job come down to a small set of drivers. For a full bathroom those drivers are layout, the hot-water system and the finish level. For a wet room they are floor construction and whether the room is an adaptation for a disabled user. The default kit is: 12.5 mm moisture-resistant plasterboard on 38x63 CLS studs, XPS or cement backer board plus liquid tanking in the shower zone, a close-coupled rimless soft-close WC, a 550 mm pedestal basin, a 1700x700 single-ended acrylic bath, a chrome mono basin mixer, a thermostatic bar mixer (combi or unvented homes) or an 8.5 kW electric shower (gravity or unknown systems), copper and compression on exposed runs, McAlpine-type 32/40 mm traps, a rigid pan connector, 300x600 tiles on flexible S1 powder adhesive with flexible grout, a 100 mm humidistat-plus-timer fan, IP65 fire-rated LED downlights and a plumbed 500x800 chrome towel rail. The evidence behind "most used" is mostly **indirect**: regulation, retailer statements, catalogue counts and review counts. No paid market data (AMA, Mintel, BMA) was accessible, and the only quantified shares for shower type and pipe material come from **2007**. Regulation and compatibility rules force more of the kit than preference does. Tanking in showers, powder adhesive for porcelain and floors, extract rates, electric-shower circuits and pressure-matched valves are rules, not choices, and the wizard should derive them rather than ask. On wizard design, the research finds strong evidence that defaults move choices (meta-analytic d ≈ 0.68). UK government research found that pre-selection made buyers 60-70% more likely to pick the pricier option. The ethical default is therefore the evidenced most-used, compliant option, never the premium one. All seed data built from this report is **synthetic and illustrative**, prices are dated observations from 2026-10-06, market values belong in `profiles/uk.yaml`, and whether buyers want a kit wizard at all is unproven.

## Most-used evidence is indirect, and two key shares date from 2007

How strong each "most used" label is decides how much a default can be trusted. The evidence falls into four grades, used in the table below. **A** is a regulation, standard or manufacturer system specification, which forces or defines the choice. **B** is merchant behaviour data: review counts, bestseller order and category counts at Screwfix or Wickes. **C** is a single retailer's statement or "best seller" label, or a price read only from a search snippet. **D** is a forum post, an unverified listicle, a manufacturer-funded survey, or data older than 2015.

The most-used labels with the strongest evidence are the screws, plugs, traps, adhesive and tiles, where merchant review counts give a clear signal. On Screwfix's plasterboard-screw category, the top three items are Easydrive 3.5 mm bugle drywall screws in 38 mm, 25 mm and 32 mm, with 236-687 reviews each ([Screwfix](https://www.screwfix.com/c/screws-nails-fixings/plasterboard-screws/cat840050)). Fischer DuoPower 6x30 has 449 reviews ([Screwfix](https://www.screwfix.com/c/screws-nails-fixings/plasterboard-wall-plugs/cat12000001)). The McAlpine 32 mm tubular P-trap has 381 reviews, and McAlpine holds 82 of 181 trap lines ([Screwfix](https://www.screwfix.com/c/heating-plumbing/traps/cat831610)). **Mapei Mapeker Rapid Set Flexible S1 has 669 reviews at £19/20 kg** ([Wickes](https://www.wickes.co.uk/Products/Tiles/Tile-Adhesives/c/1000877)). Metro white gloss 200x100 has **863 reviews**, the most of any Wickes bathroom tile ([Wickes](https://www.wickes.co.uk/Products/Tiles/Bathroom-Wall+Floor-Tiles/c/1000801)). Review counts measure engagement at one merchant, not national share, but they are the best behavioural signal found.

Sanitaryware rests on retailer statements and catalogue composition. Victorian Plumbing says close-coupled WCs are supplied "more than any other type" and are "the go-to choice in most UK homes". In its close-coupled range, 253 listings have soft-close seats against 48 with standard seats, and 132 of about 310 are rimless ([Victorian Plumbing](https://www.victorianplumbing.co.uk/buying-guides/toilets/close-coupled-toilets)). Several retailers give 1700x700 as the standard bath ([Sanctuary](https://www.sanctuary-bathrooms.co.uk/blog/understanding-bath-sizes/)), and Drench says UK retailers sell more acrylic than steel baths ([Drench](https://www.drench.co.uk/blog/buying-guides/acrylic-or-steel-baths)). Wall-hung demand comes only from a **Geberit-funded survey** of stated preference: 73% said a wall-hung WC as standard in a new home would appeal ([KBB Focus](https://kbbfocus.com/news/5341-new-research-from-geberit-highlights-trend-for-wall-hung-wcs)). That is grade D for "most used", and wall-hung belongs in premium.

Two important defaults rest on data from 2007. The only split of installed shower types is an MTP estimate of **46% electric, 38% mixer and 16% pumped**, quoted in a 2007 United Utilities/Waterwise report ([UU/Waterwise 2007](https://database.waterwise.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2019/09/United-Utilities_Water-and-Energy-Efficient-Showers_Project-Report.pdf)). The only installer pipe survey found **78% of IPHE members preferring copper**, and it is also from 2007 ([H&V News](https://www.heatingandventilating.net/pipes-and-fittings-survey-finds-copper-is-installers-choice)). A claim that electric showers are 50% of new sales came from a ShowerDoc blog that returned 403, so it is unverified. Triton's "number one showering brand" is self-description ([Norcros](https://www.norcros.com/index.php?cID=133)). Electric shower kW is a **direct conflict**. QS Supplies calls 8.5 kW "the UK's most popular wattage" ([QS Supplies](https://www.qssupplies.co.uk/how-to-choose-the-right-electric-shower-for-your-home.html)). The Wickes range lists 16 units at 9.5 kW against 7 at 8.5 kW, yet its most-reviewed units are the 8.5 kW Triton T80 (151 reviews) and Riba (133) ([Wickes](https://www.wickes.co.uk/Products/Bathrooms/Showers-Enclosures/Showers/Electric-Showers/c/1001035)). A listicle claim that the Mira Sport 9.8 kW is the best seller could not be traced to any source. The kW default should therefore follow the existing circuit, not popularity.

Social-housing specifications disagree with retail on several items, and the disagreement matters because social landlords are a likely buyer segment. A Welsh landlord's November 2025 bathroom specification lists **pillar taps, an 8.5 kW electric shower, a 1700x750 slip-resistant bath, a SELV fan, R10 safety vinyl and a TMV3 pre-set to 43°C** ([Sell2Wales 376193](https://www.sell2wales.gov.wales/Assets/NoticeBuilder_FileDownload.aspx?id=376193)). Camden lists a **steel** 1700x700 bath, though that comes from a snippet of a PDF that returned 503 ([Camden](https://camdocs.camden.gov.uk/CMWebDrawer/Record/10566578/file/document)). Retail defaults point the other way, to mono mixers, acrylic, a mains-voltage axial fan and tiles. These are two legitimate default sets for two buyer types, which argues for making `buyer_segment` (retail/trade vs social landlord) a profile or customer-standard switch rather than averaging the two.

Three readings of the table matter for the product. First, for roughly a third of the rows (tanking, primer, adhesive class, backer system, traps, extract rate, electric-shower circuit, TMV), the "default" is really a rule output, and the user should never see it as a choice. Second, budget is sometimes **not acceptable**: there is no compliant budget tanking, and MR plasterboard is not an acceptable shower substrate where NHBC applies. The wizard must hide those options in that context rather than show them as cheaper. Third, the price bands are point observations from one or two merchants on one day, several from snippets. They belong in dated, sourced profile data or supplier quotes, never in code.

## Rules and compatibility force a third of the kit

Several hard constraints remove choices outright. **Tanking**: BS 5385-1:2018 says substrates in wet areas "should be protected with a suitable proprietary tanking membrane system", either sheet or liquid, and water-resistant adhesives or grouts are not a substitute ([Palace Chemicals](https://palacechemicals.co.uk/dont-get-wet-understanding-tanking/)). **Substrate**: NHBC 9.2/06 rules out gypsum plasterboard or plaster as a tile substrate in wet rooms and power-shower bathrooms, bans magnesium oxide boards, and requires proof that the whole system is waterproof ([Schluter NHBC guidance](https://eu.schluter.com/statics/www_schlueter_gb_2x/downloads/cumulus/D00460_Schlueter_Systems_NHBC_Guidance_UK.pdf)). Strictly, NHBC binds new homes it warrants, not private refurbishment. Treating it as the refurb default is a defensible good-practice choice, not a legal requirement, and the report flags that distinction. **Adhesive**: ready-mixed tubs "cannot be used to tile floors", and porcelain or tiles over 30x30 cm need powder ([Topps Tiles, snippet](https://www.toppstiles.co.uk/knowledge-base/what-tile-adhesive-to-use)). Plywood or timber floors need S1, or S2 above 400x400 ([Weber](https://www.uk.weber/tile-fixing/tiling-timber-or-non-tongue-and-groove-floorboards-and-chipboard)). Gypsum and anhydrite need a primer to prevent the sulphate reaction with cement adhesive ([Victorian Plumbing, Primer G](https://www.victorianplumbing.co.uk/mapei-primer-g)).

The services rules are equally binding. **Ventilation**: Approved Document F sets 15 l/s intermittent or 8 l/s continuous extract for a bathroom and 6 l/s for sanitary accommodation. In rooms with no openable window, controls must run at least 15 minutes after the room is vacated ([ADF Vol 1](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/61deba42d3bf7f054fcc243d/ADF1.pdf)). A 2026 ADF edition exists for buildings under the 2026 standards, so profile values need an edition field. **Electric showers**: Triton requires a 30 mA RCD on all UK electric and pumped shower circuits, a 45 A double-pole isolator with a 3 mm contact gap and an MCB sized by kW, and "strongly recommend[s]" 10 mm² cable ([Triton T70Z](https://showerspares.com/downloads/triton-t70z-electric-shower-7.5kw-white-sp7007z.pdf)). Work in a special location such as a bathroom is notifiable under Part P in England ([AD P 2013](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/441872/BR_PDF_AD_P_2013.pdf)). Scotland has no Part P, so that flag is jurisdictional. **Hot-water compatibility**: power showers must not go on combi or unvented systems, HP/Combi valves need pressurised hot water, and gravity systems need LP-rated valves and taps or a pump. **Temperature**: Part G caps bath outlets in new dwellings at 48°C ([Homebuilding & Renovating](https://www.homebuilding.co.uk/self-build/approved-document-g)). The Welsh landlord pre-sets TMV3 to 43°C and Walsall's adaptation spec blends to 38°C ([Walsall DFG](https://go.walsall.gov.uk/sites/default/files/2022-08/specification_for_dfg_low_level_tray-3.pdf)). These are three values for three contexts, and all belong in the profile keyed to job variant. **Accessibility**: Approved Document M M4(3) requires a wet room for any installed level-access shower, a floor gully, walls able to take 1.5 kN/m², a WC pan at least 400 mm high, a front lever flush on the transfer side and a wall-hung basin with rim at 770-850 mm ([ADM Vol 1](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5a7f8a82ed915d74e622b17b/BR_PDF_AD_M1_2015_with_2016_amendments_V3.pdf)).

Compatibility rules generate lines rather than ask about them. A 32 mm basin trap and a 40 mm bath trap follow from the appliance. The tap connector follows from the tap tails. Board system screws and washers follow from the board. A pan connector follows from outlet geometry, and a wall-hung frame ships its own bend, so it needs none. A separate shaver socket is dropped when the mirror includes one. Flexible grout follows flexible adhesive, and UFH implies flexible adhesive, porcelain and an electrician. Trim depth follows tile plus bed thickness. Nothing goes on compression olives, per Pegler ([diynot](https://www.diynot.com/diy/goto/post?id=1363418)).

## Defaults work, which is exactly why they need guardrails

The evidence that defaults change behaviour is among the strongest in decision science. A meta-analysis of 58 studies (N = 73,675) found an average effect of **d = 0.68**, stronger in consumer domains, working through implied endorsement and endowment ([Jachimowicz et al. 2019](https://ideas.repec.org/a/cup/bpubpo/v3y2019i02p159-186_00.html)). The CMA calls defaults "one of the strongest and most reliable practices that influence consumer behaviour". It found a pre-selected option is on average **27% more likely** to be chosen out of two ([CMA 2022](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/624c27c68fa8f527710aaf58/Online_choice_architecture_discussion_paper.pdf)). In product configurators the default acts as a reference point. Herrmann et al. found that moving a feature's default from the lowest to the highest level raised sales, and that a sales-maximising default sits "between the medium and highest price level" ([Herrmann et al. 2011](https://search.usi.ch/en/publications/6899)). The UK Department for Business and Trade found defaults on 49% of 558 sampled consumer journeys, with 64% steering to cheaper or similarly priced options. In its choice experiment (n = 5,889), though, pre-selection made consumers **60-70% more likely to pick the more expensive option** ([DBT 2024](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/the-prevalence-and-potential-harm-of-defaults-in-online-shopping)). A sourcing agent that earns on order value would face the exact incentive Herrmann describes. The research says to refuse it: the default must be the evidenced most-used, compliant option, and the premium tier is never pre-selected.

Tiering cuts both ways. A middle option gains share just by being in the middle (the compromise effect, [Simonson 1989](https://cognition.aau.at/bg/BA/Simonson%20%281989%29.pdf)). An asymmetrically dominated decoy raises the share of the option it flatters ([Huber, Payne & Puto 1982](https://people.duke.edu/~jch8/bio/Papers/HuberPaynePutoJMR%202014.pdf)). The CMA lists "choice overload and decoys" among practices that are almost always harmful, alongside drip pricing and sludge. Defaults are listed as harmful only in some circumstances ([CMA 2022](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/624c27c68fa8f527710aaf58/Online_choice_architecture_discussion_paper.pdf)). Three tiers are legitimate here only if each tier is a real, evidenced product band from the table above, not a filler option built to make the middle look reasonable. The research found **no peer-reviewed source establishing three options as optimal**. "Three" is convention plus the compromise effect, and only a blog recommends three to five ([atticusli](https://atticusli.com/blog/posts/paradox-of-choice-digital-products/)). Choice overload itself is contested. The classic jam study (about 30% buying from 6 jars vs about 3% from 24) comes from a secondary summary ([Postrel](https://vpostrel.com/articles/indecision-making)), and the Scheibehenne meta-analysis found a near-zero mean effect. Chernev's meta-analysis reconciles them: overload appears when the choice set is complex, the task is hard, preferences are uncertain and the user wants to minimise effort. Each of those describes a homeowner or a housing officer specifying a bathroom.

Practitioner guidance turns this into a design. NN/g recommends wizards for infrequent configuration tasks, with a visible step list, exit-and-resume, help beside the wizard and previous answers reused as defaults ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/wizards/)). It also recommends progressive disclosure at no more than two levels ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/progressive-disclosure/)). Microsoft's guidance says consumer wizards should be short, with "pre-selected defaults for as many options as possible" ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/previous-versions/ms997609(v=msdn.10))). The CMA adds the ethical limits. Defaults must be "designed to be easily changed". Harm grows with sludge, forced outcomes, framing the non-default as risky, or presenting acceptance as the norm. Pre-ticked consent boxes are a recognised remedy target ([CMA 2022](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/624c27c68fa8f527710aaf58/Online_choice_architecture_discussion_paper.pdf)). In practice that means five commitments. **Paid add-ons are opt-in**: UFH, dual-fuel kits, LED mirrors and the extra-cost premium fan stay unticked. **The total includes VAT from the first screen**, and any labour or electrician implications (Part P, chasing for concealed valves) are shown up front rather than dripped in later. **A "most used" badge appears only where the evidence grade is B or better**, with the reason visible ("meets Approved Document F; 669 reviews at one merchant"); grade C and D defaults are labelled "our standard pick" instead. **Rule-forced lines are explained, not offered** ("Tanking included: BS 5385-1 recommends it in showers"), and lines a rule excludes are not shown at all. **The premium tier is a deliberate opt-in**, never a reference-point default, and the middle tier must not be propped up by a decoy.

The repo's hard rules line up with this. A wizard default is a template assumption, so its provenance should be `default_template`, and under R3 it can never satisfy a critical attribute. The user or a supplier quote has to confirm it. The wizard's tier words should not reuse "Tier A". In the product spec, Tier A refers to approved part candidates for substitution control (R2). Calling wizard levels "budget / standard / premium" avoids a confusion that could leak into PO logic.

Room dimensions are an input for quantities, not a preference question, so the user can draw or type them on a separate measure step. The "don't know" answer to the hot-water question is safe by design. It routes to an electric shower and LP-or-universal taps, which fit any home ([UU/Waterwise 2007](https://database.waterwise.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2019/09/United-Utilities_Water-and-Energy-Efficient-Showers_Project-Report.pdf)). It also raises the electrician flag, so the cost of not knowing is visible rather than hidden. In the wet-room scope, the adaptation question carries the most weight of any question in the wizard. It changes about a dozen lines and the outlet temperature, and the Walsall and Welsh specifications show those lines are standard in DFG work.

## Conclusion

The research reframes the wizard. It is not a product picker; it is a rule engine with a short questionnaire on top. About a third of the kit is forced by standards and compatibility, and most of the rest has a defensible single default. The real decision drivers number three or four per scope. The weak point is the word "most". Outside fixings, traps, adhesive and tiles, "most used" rests on retailer statements, snippet prices and two surveys from 2007. Labelling those defaults as popular would itself be a misleading claim under the CMA framing, and the repo forbids marketing claims anyway. The honest label is "standard pick, because...", with the reason attached.

Before any of this ships, three pieces of work would move evidence grades the most. Sort-by-bestseller and review-count captures at Screwfix, Toolstation and Wickes for showers, valves, fans and backer boards would lift several rows from C to B. A primary read of NHBC 9.2/06 would settle its date and screw centres. Two or three more social-landlord specifications would test whether `buyer_segment` really splits the defaults as the Welsh document suggests. The commercial hypothesis is still the larger uncertainty: whether trades or housing officers will accept a three-question kit rather than paste their own list is untested, and a single bathroom template with a gold-kit evaluation is the cheapest way to find out.
